Go-to-market strategy

Account-based go-to-market. Fewer accounts, built to close.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.18 November 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a Dubai-based revenue operations and go-to-market consultant for founder-led B2B teams. More on her background at the about section.

Account-based go-to-market is a model where sales, marketing and customer success agree on a named list of target accounts and work it together, rather than marketing handing sales a stream of leads to sort through. The account is the unit of planning, outreach and measurement, not the individual lead, and the list usually runs in one of three tiers depending on how many accounts it covers.

The short answer. The account is the unit, not the lead.

Account-based go-to-market flips the usual order of operations. A traditional funnel generates as many leads as a budget allows, then filters down to the ones worth a sales conversation. An account-based model picks the companies worth winning first, then builds marketing, outreach and sales effort specifically for that list. Nobody outside the list gets worked; nobody on the list gets ignored because a lead score missed them.

This matters most where deal value is concentrated and the buying decision involves several people rather than one. A single enterprise account with a six-person buying committee is worth more sustained, coordinated effort than fifty cold leads that might contain one buyer. Demand Gen Report's 2026 Account-Based Marketing Benchmark Survey found that 56% of B2B marketers now name new account acquisition as the primary goal of their ABM programme, with a further 28% focused on expanding existing accounts, and close to half integrate their demand generation and account-based processes into one motion rather than running them as separate functions.

The three standard tiers, borrowed from the earliest ABM frameworks and still the clearest way to plan one, are one-to-one, one-to-few and one-to-many. One-to-one treats each named account as its own campaign, researched and messaged individually. One-to-few groups similar accounts, usually by industry or use case, and runs one tailored campaign per cluster. One-to-many applies account-based targeting and personalisation at a broader scale, closer to segmented demand generation than bespoke account work. Most founder-led teams should start at one-to-one or one-to-few. One-to-many needs tooling and volume that rarely suits an early-stage commercial team.

What makes it a go-to-market model rather than just a targeting tactic is the agreement behind it. Sales and marketing sign off on the same list before any campaign runs, and both are measured against it. Without that shared list, account-based activity is just better-targeted advertising sitting next to a sales team working its own pipeline.

Not every business should run this model. It earns its effort where the sales cycle is long, the contract value is high enough to justify research time per account, and the addressable market is genuinely concentrated, a few hundred or a few thousand real-fit accounts rather than tens of thousands of similar small buyers. A business selling a low-cost, high-volume product with a short cycle gets little from account-based go-to-market; the per-account research cost never pays back against a deal that size. Most of the Middle East hospitality and enterprise SaaS engagements I see fit the first profile well, because the buying committees are genuinely large and the deals genuinely slow.

How it works in practice. Three tiers, one shared list.

TierTypical account countWhat it looks like
One-to-one10 to 50 named accountsIndividually researched messaging, a named account owner, custom sales collateral where the deal justifies it
One-to-few50 to 300 accounts in clustersOne campaign per cluster (by industry, trigger event or use case), personalised at the cluster level rather than per account
One-to-many300+ accountsScaled personalisation using firmographic and intent data, closer to segmented demand generation than hand-built outreach

Building the list starts with fit, not interest. Score every candidate account against your actual ideal-customer profile, firm size, sector, tech stack, growth stage, whatever has historically correlated with a good outcome, before layering on any intent signal such as hiring activity or website visits. A high-intent account that is a poor fit is still a poor fit; intent data should narrow a qualified list, not replace the qualification step.

Once the list exists, the harder work is mapping the buying committee inside each account. Most B2B deals above a modest size involve a champion, a budget holder, at least one technical or operational evaluator, and often a procurement or legal gatekeeper. A one-to-one programme that only has a relationship with one of those four people is working a lead, not an account, however good the list looks on paper.

Building the account list well means combining three kinds of data rather than relying on any one of them alone. Firmographic data (size, sector, geography) sets the outer boundary of fit. CRM win and loss history adds the pattern a generic ideal-customer profile misses, the specific traits that have actually correlated with a closed-won deal versus a stalled one in your own pipeline, not a textbook persona. Intent signals, hiring activity in a relevant function, a recent funding round, a leadership change, then tell you which fit accounts are worth prioritising this quarter rather than next. Teams that skip the CRM history step and build a list purely from firmographic filters and third-party intent data tend to end up with a list that looks right on paper and underperforms in practice, because it was never checked against what has actually worked before.

The orchestration step is where most programmes fail before they start. Sales outreach, marketing content and, where budget allows, targeted advertising all need to hit the same named accounts in a coordinated sequence, not as three separate efforts that happen to share a spreadsheet. A simple weekly check-in between the account owner and marketing, reviewing which accounts moved and which content landed, does more for a founder-led team than any platform.

What good looks like. A worked scenario on a list that actually moved.

A B2B software team I worked with, illustrative rather than a named client, had been running outbound against a list of around four hundred mid-market accounts with no tiering and no shared ownership between sales and marketing. Reply rates were low, the sales team was frustrated chasing contacts with no budget authority, and marketing's content calendar had no relationship to which accounts sales actually cared about that month.

We cut the four hundred down to twenty-five tier-one accounts using three filters: a minimum deal-size threshold based on the team's actual cost to serve, a recent hiring signal in a relevant function, and an existing warm contact somewhere in the organisation, even a weak one. Those twenty-five got individual research briefs, a named sales owner, and marketing content built specifically to the trigger that had got them on the list in the first place, rather than generic category content.

The remaining accounts didn't disappear; they moved into a one-to-few tier grouped by vertical, running lighter campaigns with shared messaging. Within one quarter, the twenty-five tier-one accounts had produced more sales-accepted opportunities than the previous full list of four hundred had produced over the prior two quarters, and average deal size on those opportunities was noticeably larger, because the accounts had been chosen for fit and budget rather than for ease of contact.

The lesson that generalises beyond this one team: a smaller, better-chosen list that both functions actually work together will consistently outperform a larger list that neither function fully owns. The constraint was never lead volume. It was the absence of a shared, agreed account list that both sales and marketing were accountable to.

The buying-committee mapping on those twenty-five accounts followed a simple template rather than anything elaborate: for each account, name the likely champion, the budget holder, one technical evaluator and, where the deal size justified it, the procurement contact, even before a single one of them had been spoken to. Having the template filled in, even partly guessed, made every subsequent conversation sharper, because the sales owner already knew which gaps in the committee still needed a name attached to them, rather than discovering the gaps mid-negotiation when a deal suddenly stalled on an objection from someone nobody had engaged.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where ABM quietly fails.

The first and most common failure is running account-based advertising without account-based sales. Targeted ads against a named list feel like ABM and report well on a dashboard, but if sales is still working a separate, unrelated pipeline, the two efforts never compound. The list has to be the same list in both places.

The second is building a list too large for the tier chosen. A one-to-one programme with two hundred accounts is not one-to-one; it is a one-to-many programme pretending to be bespoke, and the individual research and messaging the model depends on simply will not happen at that volume with a small team's capacity.

The third is stopping at a single contact. An account with one engaged champion and no visibility into the rest of the buying committee is still a lead-based motion wearing an account-based label. Budget approval, procurement and technical sign-off each have their own objections, and none of them get surfaced by talking to one person.

The fourth is measuring the programme on lead volume instead of account engagement and pipeline. If the reporting still counts total marketing-qualified leads generated, the incentives quietly pull back toward breadth over fit, and the tiering exercise gets undone by the metric used to judge it.

The fifth is letting the list go stale. Accounts move in and out of fit as budgets change, champions leave and competitors win deals. A list reviewed once at kickoff and never revisited will have a meaningful share of dead or disqualified accounts within two quarters, quietly wasting the coordinated effort the whole model depends on.

The sixth is building the entire programme around new logos and ignoring the accounts already signed. Demand Gen Report's 2026 ABM Benchmark Survey put account expansion second only to new acquisition as a stated programme goal, at 28%, and for good reason: an existing account already trusts the firm and already has a champion inside it, which is most of the hard work a new-logo list has to do from scratch. A roadmap that treats expansion as an afterthought for customer success to handle alone is leaving the easiest tier of the model on the table.

Common questions.

What is account-based go-to-market?

Account-based go-to-market is a model where sales, marketing and customer success agree on a defined list of target accounts and work that same list together, instead of marketing generating leads for sales to qualify separately. The account, not the individual lead, is the unit everyone plans and measures against.

How many accounts should a one-to-one ABM programme target?

Most one-to-one programmes run with somewhere between 10 and 50 named accounts, because each one gets individually researched messaging and a coordinated sales and marketing play. Beyond that range, teams usually move to a one-to-few model that groups similar accounts and builds one campaign per cluster rather than per account.

What is the difference between ABM and traditional demand generation?

Demand generation casts wide and qualifies whoever responds, measuring success in leads and marketing-qualified leads. Account-based go-to-market starts from a fixed account list chosen before any campaign runs, and measures success in account engagement and pipeline created within that list, not in total lead volume.

Do we need a dedicated ABM platform to start?

No. A founder-led team can run a credible one-to-few programme on a shared spreadsheet, a CRM list view and manually built outreach sequences. Dedicated ABM platforms earn their cost once a team is managing intent data and ad orchestration across more than roughly fifty accounts at once.

How do you measure ABM success?

Track account-level engagement (how many people in the buying committee have taken a meaningful action), pipeline value created within the named list, and win rate for accounts worked through the programme against accounts that were not. A rising lead count outside the named list is not a success metric for ABM.

Is ABM suitable for a small, founder-led team?

Yes, and it often suits a small team better than a large one, because a founder-led business usually cannot afford to chase a long tail of poor-fit leads. A tight list of twenty to forty well-researched accounts, worked properly, tends to outperform a broad outbound list run at the same effort.
