Customer journey mapping

The awareness stage of the customer journey. Where buyers stop needing you to find them.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.11 November 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a revenue operations and customer journey consultant for founder-led teams. More on her background at the about section.

The awareness stage is the first part of the customer journey, the point where a prospect realises they have a problem and starts looking for information about it, before they know which companies might solve it. Most of this happens with no sales contact at all, which is why what gets published here matters more than what gets pitched.

The short answer. A problem gets named before a product does.

When I map a customer journey with a client, the awareness stage is almost always described wrong on the first attempt. Teams tend to draw it as "sees our ad" or "finds our website", as if awareness begins the moment a prospect encounters the business. It doesn't. Awareness begins earlier, when someone notices a problem is costing them time, money or confidence, and starts trying to understand it, usually with no idea yet that a company like theirs exists to help.

That distinction matters because it changes what a business should be publishing. If awareness starts with "finds us", the instinct is to spend on visibility: ads, outreach, a louder brand. If awareness actually starts with "names the problem", the job is different: be the clearest, most useful explanation of that problem a searching person can find, long before they're ready to evaluate a solution, let alone a vendor.

Gartner's own research backs how far this self-directed research now runs before anyone wants a conversation. A Gartner survey of 646 B2B buyers, fielded between August and September 2025, found that 67% said they would prefer a completely sales-rep-free buying experience if one were available. A separate Gartner page covering similar ground cites 75% instead, which is worth flagging rather than quietly picking whichever number sounds better, since even Gartner's own materials don't agree on the precise figure. What both numbers agree on is the direction: most buyers want to do the early thinking alone, and the awareness stage is where almost all of that thinking happens.

How it works in practice. Three signals that someone is still at the awareness stage.

Not every early-stage visitor looks the same, but three signals show up consistently enough to plan around.

SignalWhat it looks likeWhat it means for content
Problem-framed search, no brand terms"why do my sales reports never match" rather than "CRM dashboard tool"They haven't yet named the category of solution; a product pitch here reads as premature
Long time on page, high bounceThey read thoroughly, then leave without clicking anything commercialThey came to understand, not to buy; a hard CTA mid-article will usually be ignored
Questions aimed at an AI assistant, not a search barFull sentences, context included, phrased as if asking a personThe content needs a direct, self-contained answer near the top, since that's what gets lifted into the reply

The first signal is the one founder-led teams misread most often. A prospect searching "why do my sales reports never match" has a real, specific, costly problem, and is a long way from typing "CRM comparison" or a competitor's name. Meeting them with a feature list at this point doesn't fail because the content is bad; it fails because it answers a question they haven't asked yet. The content that earns attention here explains the mechanism behind the problem, why reports from the CRM, the spreadsheet and the finance system disagree, in terms a non-specialist would use to describe it themselves.

The second signal is measurable and often ignored. A page with unusually long time-on-page and a high bounce rate isn't necessarily failing; if the visitor arrived with a problem-framed query and leaves having genuinely understood their situation, that's the awareness stage doing its job, even though no form got filled in. Judging every page by its conversion rate alone penalises exactly the content that's doing the most good this early, and most analytics setups don't separate "left because it was unhelpful" from "left because they got what they needed and weren't ready to buy yet".

The third signal is newer and growing. Questions typed into an AI assistant tend to arrive with more context than a search engine query, because the asker expects a conversational answer rather than a list of links. A direct, self-contained answer placed early in the content, one that would make sense lifted out and read on its own, is what gets surfaced back to that person, whether or not they ever visit the page itself.

Most analytics setups weren't built to separate these three signals from the metrics that matter at later stages, and that's usually the real reason awareness-stage content gets deprioritised in a quarterly review. A marketing dashboard built around conversions and demo requests will show awareness-stage pages as expensive to maintain and light on return, because it's measuring them against a job they were never meant to do. Tagging content by the stage it's written for, even with something as simple as a spreadsheet column, and reviewing each stage against its own metric, time on page and return visits for awareness, conversion rate for consideration and decision, stops a genuinely working awareness-stage page from being cut because it was judged on the wrong number.

What good looks like. A worked scenario on content built for the wrong stage.

A SaaS founder I worked with, illustrative rather than a named client, had built a solid library of comparison pages, "us versus competitor A", "us versus competitor B", and was puzzled that organic traffic stayed flat despite the effort. Looking at the actual search terms bringing people to the site, almost none of them were comparison searches. The real traffic was arriving on problem-framed terms the company had never written for directly, people trying to understand why their trial sign-ups weren't converting, not people comparing named tools to solve it.

The comparison content wasn't wrong, it was aimed at the wrong stage of the same journey. Prospects comparing named tools are already deep into consideration; the company had built plenty of content for a stage most of its actual visitors hadn't reached yet, and almost nothing for the stage they were actually in. The fix was to write a smaller number of awareness-stage pieces first, each answering one specific, problem-framed question in plain language, with no product mention until the final paragraph, then let the existing comparison pages pick visitors up once they'd worked out what category of answer they needed.

The decision rule worth keeping from that process: write the awareness-stage piece as if the product doesn't exist yet, then add one honest paragraph at the end about how it fits in. If removing every mention of the product would still leave a genuinely useful piece of writing, it's doing its job at this stage. If it falls apart without the product in it, it was written for consideration, not awareness, whatever stage of the journey the brief claimed to target.

Within two quarters of making that shift, organic traffic on problem-framed terms roughly doubled, and a noticeable share of new trial sign-ups started arriving through brand search, people who had read an awareness-stage piece weeks earlier, gone away to think, and come back by name once they'd worked out what kind of solution they needed. That delayed path is easy to miss in a last-click attribution model, which is itself a reason awareness-stage work gets under-credited and, eventually, under-funded, even when it's genuinely driving later pipeline.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where awareness-stage content quietly fails.

The first pitfall is leading with the product instead of the problem. A prospect who hasn't named their problem yet has no frame for evaluating a solution to it, and content that opens with "introducing our platform" gets skipped before the actual question gets answered, if it ever does.

The second is measuring this stage by the wrong metric. Awareness-stage content that converts immediately is rare and shouldn't be the bar; the right measure is whether it ranks, gets read in full, and earns a return visit or a brand search later, once the prospect has moved on to naming solutions.

The third is assuming every visitor is close to buying and pushing a demo or a pricing page too early. A hard CTA in the first few paragraphs of a genuinely awareness-stage piece reads as a mismatch between what the prospect came for and what the page is trying to extract, and it costs more trust than the occasional early lead is worth.

The fourth is writing the direct-answer block for search engines but not for how a person would actually ask the question out loud. A generative engine lifts the most self-contained, plainly worded answer it can find, not the one stuffed with the keyword in every sentence, so the awareness-stage content most likely to surface in an AI answer is usually the plainest, not the most optimised-looking.

The fifth, specific to founder-led teams, is treating the awareness stage as marketing's problem alone. The founder or the person closest to the client relationship usually understands the real, messy, specific way a prospect first describes their problem better than anyone briefing the content from a keyword list, and that first-hand phrasing is exactly what the best awareness-stage content needs to borrow.

The sixth is assuming the awareness stage only matters for organic search. The same mismatch shows up in paid campaigns and sales outreach too, an ad or a cold email that opens with a product pitch to someone who has never named the underlying problem will underperform one that opens with the problem itself, for exactly the same reason a comparison page underperforms with a reader who hasn't decided what category of answer they need yet. The channel changes; the sequencing a prospect actually needs does not.

Common questions.

What is the awareness stage of the customer journey?

The awareness stage is the point where a prospect first recognises they have a problem worth solving and starts looking for information about it, before they know which companies might solve it or whether they are ready to buy. It ends once they can name the category of solution they need.

How long does the awareness stage usually last?

It varies by how urgent and expensive the problem is. A founder searching for why their sales pipeline keeps stalling might spend weeks reading before naming the problem as a CRM or process gap, while a more acute issue, a system outage or a lost client, can compress the stage into a single afternoon.

Do buyers want to talk to sales during the awareness stage?

Mostly not yet. Gartner's survey of 646 B2B buyers, fielded in August and September 2025, found 67% said they would prefer a sales-rep-free buying experience if they could have one. A separate Gartner page on the same topic cites 75%, so treat the exact figure as directional rather than a settled number, but the direction, a strong preference for self-directed research early on, is consistent.

What content works best at the awareness stage?

Content that answers the question a prospect is actually typing before they know your product category exists, not content that assumes they already know they need you. A plain explanation of the problem, written in the prospect's own words, consistently outperforms an early pitch for a specific solution.

How is the awareness stage different from the consideration stage?

At the awareness stage, a prospect is naming a problem; at the consideration stage, they are comparing named solutions to it, including yours. Pushing a comparison, a demo offer or a pricing page onto someone still in the awareness stage usually gets ignored, because they have not yet decided what kind of answer they are looking for.

How do I know if my content is reaching people at the awareness stage?

Check which search terms and questions actually bring people to a page, not which terms you wrote it for. Problem-framed, low-intent searches without a brand or product name attached are the clearest sign of awareness-stage traffic, and they usually arrive with a higher bounce rate and a longer time on page than bottom-of-funnel visits.
