Go-to-market strategy

A channel-led go-to-market strategy. Growth sold through someone else's customer relationship.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.9 November 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a go-to-market and growth consultant for founder-led and GCC-expanding teams. More on her background at the about section.

A channel-led go-to-market strategy routes sales through third parties, resellers, referral partners, technology alliances, rather than a direct sales team or self-serve sign-up. It works when partners have a real reason to sell your product ahead of anything else in their portfolio, which means protected margin, fast deal registration and ongoing enablement, not a signed agreement left to run itself.

The short answer. A partner sells, or at least introduces, on your behalf.

A channel-led go-to-market strategy sits alongside, or instead of, a direct sales motion and a self-serve product-led one. Rather than your own reps closing the deal, a third party does some or all of that work: a reseller who takes the product to market under their own brand, a referral partner who introduces a warm lead and steps back, or a technology alliance partner who co-sells where the two products integrate. You build the product, the pricing and the partner programme that supports them. The partner brings the relationship, and often the trust, that gets you into an account you couldn't reach as quickly alone.

The appeal is obvious on paper: one partner can bring the reach of several reps without the fixed cost of hiring them. The catch is that a partner's attention is finite and shared across every other product in their portfolio, which means a channel strategy that doesn't give a partner a concrete reason to prioritise you over the next vendor on their list will simply sit there, signed but dormant. A 2026 Forrester survey of partner ecosystem marketing decision-makers found that more than half now say 41% or more of their organisation's revenue comes through indirect routes-to-market and partners, a meaningful share of overall growth for companies that get the mechanics right, and exactly the kind of number that looks encouraging in a board deck while hiding how unevenly it's often distributed across partners who are actually active.

Where a channel motion sits on that spectrum, from a handful of informal referral relationships to a fully built reseller network with its own enablement team, should follow the product's price point and sales complexity, not ambition alone. A low-price, self-explanatory product travels well through referral and affiliate partners, who need almost no training to describe it. A complex, high-price enterprise sale asks far more of a reseller, who effectively needs to run your own sales process on your behalf, and building that capability in a partner who has never sold anything like it before usually takes longer than most GTM plans allow for.

How it works in practice. The three partner types, and what each one actually needs.

Partner typeWhat they doWhat they need from you
Resellers and distributorsResell under their own brand or alongside their own services, often owning the full commercial relationshipProtected margin, registered deal protection, and a steady supply of qualified leads
Referral and affiliate partnersIntroduce a warm lead and step back, rarely touching the deal againA fast, visible payout and a handoff that takes minutes, not a form that disappears into a queue
Technology and ISV alliancesCo-sell where the two products already integrate, often to a shared customer baseJoint collateral, a named point of contact on your side, and an integration that genuinely works without constant support tickets

Resellers and distributors are the heaviest-weight channel relationship, because they're carrying your product alongside their own commercial risk. They need deal registration: a formal process where they log an opportunity before working it, in exchange for protected margin and the assurance that your own direct team, or a competing partner, won't swoop in on the same account once it's clearly progressing. Without that protection, a reseller has every incentive to under-invest time in deals they might lose at the finish line to someone else.

Referral and affiliate partners want the opposite of complexity. They're not running a sales process on your behalf, they're spending a small amount of social or professional capital to make an introduction, and the moment that introduction becomes slow or awkward to act on, they stop bothering. A referral that sits in an unmonitored inbox for three days teaches the partner, correctly, that referring you isn't worth their time.

Technology and ISV alliances live or die on whether the integration actually works day to day, not on how it looked in the partnership announcement. A co-sell motion built on an integration that breaks quietly, or that neither side's support team fully understands, generates support tickets instead of pipeline, which is the fastest way to turn an enthusiastic alliance partner cold. This is the same discipline behind our growth consulting work: a channel only compounds growth once the mechanics underneath it are boring and reliable, not while they're still being worked out live on a customer call.

Not every partner in a programme deserves the same investment, and treating them as if they do is a common way channel budgets get wasted. A small number of partners, the ones who've already registered deals or produced real pipeline, are worth enablement calls, co-marketing budget and a named contact on your side. A long tail of partners who signed an agreement and never acted on it are better served by a lighter-touch, mostly automated relationship, a partner portal, self-serve collateral, until or unless they show signs of actually becoming active. Spreading enablement effort evenly across both groups usually means the active partners get less attention than they've earned.

What good looks like. A worked scenario on partner concentration risk.

A hospitality technology vendor I worked with, illustrative rather than a named client, had built a genuinely channel-led go-to-market motion, and on the surface it looked healthy: pipeline was growing quarter over quarter, and the team had stopped worrying about direct outbound almost entirely. The underlying picture was less comfortable. A single large regional reseller accounted for the majority of that channel pipeline, and nobody had been tracking revenue by named partner, only the lump "channel" total on the board slide. When that reseller reorganised its own sales team and quietly deprioritised the product in favour of a competing line they'd just picked up, pipeline from that source collapsed over the following quarter, and there was no earlier signal anyone had been watching for, because the dashboard had never broken the number down far enough to show it coming.

The decision rule put in place afterwards was specific: no single partner is allowed to represent more than roughly 40% of total channel-sourced pipeline without an active, named plan to diversify, and partner-sourced pipeline gets reviewed monthly by named partner, not as one combined channel figure. It's a deliberately uncomfortable rule to apply when one partner is performing well, since the instinct is to lean further into what's working. The point of naming the ceiling in advance is that it forces the diversification conversation before a single partner's internal reshuffle becomes the company's problem too.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where channel-led GTM quietly stalls.

The first pitfall is treating a signed partner agreement as the same thing as an active partner. A logo on a partner page and a reseller who has actually registered a deal in the last quarter are not the same relationship, and conflating the two is how a "channel strategy" ends up being three enthusiastic conversations and forty dormant agreements.

The second is skipping deal registration, or running it so slowly that partners stop bothering to use it. Without a working registration process, channel conflict becomes routine: a partner invests weeks in an account only to find the direct sales team closing the same deal from the other direction, and that partner's trust in the programme drops sharply after the first time it happens, whether or not it was deliberate.

The third is enabling partners once, at signing, and never again. Product changes, pricing updates and new competitive positioning all go stale in a partner's head the moment they're not actively selling the product every week, unlike your own reps who hear updates in a weekly team meeting. A partner enablement cadence, even a short quarterly update, keeps the pitch partners are actually giving your customers aligned with what's true today.

The fourth is compensating partners only on net-new business, which quietly discourages them from flagging renewal or churn risk on accounts they introduced. A partner who sees no benefit in raising a concern about a customer renewing has no reason to raise it early, and by the time it surfaces on your own side, the window to fix it has often already closed.

A fifth pitfall is launching a channel motion before the direct sales message is actually proven. A partner is reselling a pitch, a positioning, a set of objection-handling answers, that your own team worked out the hard way through real sales calls. Handing an unproven pitch to a partner means they're the ones discovering what doesn't land with a customer, and they'll generally stop trying well before that feedback loop makes it back to you.

A sixth, quieter pitfall is measuring channel success only by total partner-sourced revenue rather than by how many partners are genuinely active. A channel programme with 150 signed partners and a channel programme with 15 can post the identical revenue number if that revenue is concentrated in a handful of relationships either way, but the first looks far healthier on a slide than it actually is. Counting active, deal-registering partners alongside revenue is what shows whether the channel is actually broad or just lucky.

Common questions.

What is a channel-led go-to-market strategy?

A channel-led go-to-market strategy sells and delivers a product mainly through third parties, resellers, referral partners and technology alliances, rather than through a direct sales team or self-serve sign-up. The company builds the product and the partner programme; the partner does the selling, or at least the introducing.

Is channel-led GTM the same as partner-led?

They're often used interchangeably, but channel-led usually implies a partner who resells or actively sells on your behalf, while partner-led can include lighter-touch relationships, referral introductions, co-marketing, integrations, that influence a sale without the partner closing it directly. Channel-led is the narrower, sales-heavy end of partner-led.

How much of a company's revenue typically comes through channel partners?

It varies enormously by industry and company, which is why sweeping industry-wide figures are unreliable. A 2026 Forrester survey of partner ecosystem marketing decision-makers found that more than half reported 41% or more of their organisation's revenue now comes through indirect routes-to-market and partners, a meaningful share but far from universal.

What is deal registration and why does it matter?

Deal registration is a process where a partner formally logs an opportunity before working it, in exchange for protected margin and reduced risk that your own direct team, or another partner, chases the same account. Without it, partners have little incentive to invest time in a deal they might lose to someone else at the last minute.

Can a channel-led motion work alongside a direct sales team?

Yes, and most companies with a mature channel run both. The two need clear rules for who owns which accounts, by size, territory or existing relationship, agreed before conflict happens rather than negotiated deal by deal, or the direct team and the channel will end up competing for the same customer.
