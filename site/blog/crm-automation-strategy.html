CRM automation & workflow

A CRM automation strategy. Not a pile of unrelated rules.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.27 November 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a growth and CRM consultant for founder-led and SaaS teams. More on her background at the about section.

A CRM automation strategy is a written decision about which parts of the sales process run on rules rather than a person, built on one reliable data model with one named owner. Without that structure, individual automations still work, but they stop adding up to anything a team can actually explain or maintain.

The short answer. Decide the data model before the rules.

I'm Lauren Pearson, and almost every CRM I've looked into has automation in it. Almost none of them have a strategy behind that automation. The difference shows up the moment you ask someone to list everything the CRM currently does on its own, a question that usually gets a long pause, a half-remembered list, and a promise to check the settings and get back to you. That gap between "we have automation" and "we know what our automation does" is the strategy problem, and it has nothing to do with how sophisticated any individual rule is.

Salesforce's State of Sales report, seventh edition, surveying 4,050 sales professionals across 22 countries in fieldwork run August to September 2025, found 87% of sales organisations now use some form of AI or automation for tasks like prospecting and forecasting. Adoption of automation itself is no longer the gap for most teams. The gap is coherence: a strategy that ties what gets automated to one shared data model, one review cadence, and one person accountable for the whole set of rules, rather than automation arriving piecemeal as whoever hits a bottleneck that week builds a fix for it.

How it works in practice. Data model, rule inventory, ownership.

A CRM automation strategy starts with the data the automation will depend on, not the rules themselves. A lead-assignment rule built on a "lead source" field that three different teams fill in three different ways will misfire constantly, and no amount of clever rule logic fixes a field problem underneath it. Fixing field reliability, agreeing what each key field means and who is responsible for keeping it accurate, comes before building or extending a single automation.

Once the data is trustworthy, the strategy becomes a short, prioritised list: which manual tasks cost the most time or cause the most missed follow-up, in what order to automate them, and what each automation is actually meant to achieve. This is where a written rule inventory earns its keep, a simple list of every active automation, what triggers it, what it does, and who built it. Teams that skip this step tend to discover, usually during a CRM migration or a new hire's onboarding, that nobody can confidently say what half the active rules are for.

Strategy elementWhat it answers
Data modelWhich fields are reliable enough to trigger a rule on
Rule inventoryWhat is automated, by whom, and why
Review cadenceHow often rules get checked against current reality
OwnerWho is accountable for the whole set, not just one rule

Ownership is the element most founder-led teams skip, because individual rules often get built by whoever hits the problem first, a sales rep, a founder, an operations hire, with no one person tracking the accumulated total. That works fine for the first five rules. By rule twenty, nobody remembers why three of them exist, and removing one feels risky because nobody is certain what else depends on it.

Sequencing matters more than most teams expect when they start. Automate lead capture and routing before trying to automate anything downstream of it, because a routing rule built on a messy capture process just distributes the mess faster and more widely. Automate reminders and follow-up nudges before automating decisions, since a nudge that fails just means a human still makes the call a little later, while a decision rule that fails on bad data can send the wrong outcome to a customer before anyone notices. Starting with the lowest-risk, highest-friction tasks and working toward anything that touches a customer directly keeps an early mistake small and easy to unwind.

A review cadence has to be written down with an actual date, not left as a vague intention to "check in periodically". Monthly is reasonable for a small, fast-changing team; quarterly suits a more settled process. Either way, the review should ask three questions of every active rule: does the trigger field still mean what it meant when the rule was built, has the process it supports changed underneath it, and would the team build this rule again today if it did not already exist. A rule that fails the third question is usually safe to retire, whatever sunk effort went into building it originally.

What good looks like. A short list everyone can explain.

A good CRM automation strategy looks almost boring: a short, named list of active rules, each with a clear reason to exist, reviewed on a set schedule, with new rules added only after checking they do not duplicate or conflict with something already running. Nobody is surprised by what the CRM does on its own, because the full list is written down somewhere everyone involved has actually seen.

I worked with an illustrative B2B services team, not a named client, whose CRM had accumulated seventeen active automations over about two years, each added by a different person solving a specific problem at the time. Running the rule inventory exercise found four rules that no longer matched current reality, one still routing leads to a product line the business had discontinued the previous year, and two rules quietly duplicating the same reminder through different triggers, which meant some reps were getting the same nudge twice. Retiring the four stale rules and merging the duplicate pair did not add a single new feature. It just meant the fifteen remaining rules were the ones the team actually intended to be running, which is a different thing from the seventeen they had simply never got round to checking.

Good also means the strategy scales with the data, not just with ambition. A rule that assigns leads by territory works cleanly while the business sells into three regions and breaks quietly once a fourth region arrives with a different sales structure, unless someone is actually responsible for noticing the mismatch and updating the rule rather than letting it keep running against an outdated map.

It also means new hires can be handed the rule inventory on their first week and understand, without asking around, what the CRM does without being told to. A strategy that only lives in one person's head is not meaningfully different from having no strategy at all, since it disappears the day that person is unavailable, busy, or has simply moved on to a different role. Writing the inventory down, even as a single shared document rather than anything elaborate, is what turns tacit knowledge into something the business can actually rely on.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where CRM automation strategies fail.

The first and most common failure is building automation reactively, one fix at a time, with no running inventory of what already exists. Each individual rule can be sensible and still add up to a system nobody fully understands, which is the exact definition of automation without a strategy behind it.

The second is automating a broken process instead of fixing it first. A follow-up sequence that automatically chases a lead through a stage that does not reflect how the business actually sells just makes the broken process run faster and more consistently, which is worse than a slow broken process, not better.

The third is confusing automation volume with progress. Seventeen active rules sounds more advanced than five, but the number that actually matters is how many of them the team can confidently explain and would rebuild on purpose today. A strategy measured by rule count rather than rule clarity tends to drift toward exactly the kind of unmanaged pile this whole approach is meant to prevent, and it gets worse, not better, as newer AI-assisted automation tools make it faster than ever to spin up another rule without anyone pausing to check whether it duplicates one already running.

The fourth is leaving ownership with "whoever built it" rather than a single named role. When the person who built a rule leaves the business or moves teams, a rule with no clear current owner tends to either break silently or keep running long after it should have been retired, because nobody felt it was specifically their job to notice either way.

The fifth is treating the strategy as a one-off project rather than a standing discipline. A rule inventory built once during a CRM migration and never revisited drifts back toward exactly the unmanaged pile it was meant to fix, usually within a year, as new rules get added under deadline pressure and the review cadence quietly lapses once the person who championed it moves on to the next priority. The strategy only holds if the review date is treated with the same seriousness as a board meeting or a renewal date, something that happens whether or not anyone feels like doing it that week.

Our CRM implementation work starts with exactly this audit, a full rule inventory and a data model check, before adding a single new automation, because extending a strategy nobody can currently explain just produces a bigger version of the same problem.

Common questions.

What is a CRM automation strategy?

It is a written decision about which parts of the sales and customer process should run on CRM rules rather than a person, tied to one shared data model, with one owner responsible for reviewing and retiring rules. Without that structure, automation accumulates as disconnected fixes rather than a coherent system.

Is CRM automation the same as a CRM automation strategy?

No. CRM automation is the individual rules themselves, a reminder, a lead assignment, a follow-up email. A strategy is the decision about which rules to build, in what order, against which data fields, and who is accountable for checking they still make sense as the business changes.

Where should a founder-led team start a CRM automation strategy?

With the data fields the automation will depend on, not the rules themselves. A lead-assignment or follow-up rule built on a messy or inconsistently filled field will misfire constantly, no matter how well the rule logic is written, so fixing field reliability comes first.

How many CRM automations is too many?

There is no fixed number, but if nobody on the team could list what every active automation does without checking the settings, there are too many for the oversight currently in place. Rule count should track the team's actual capacity to review and retire rules, not grow freely just because the platform allows it.

Who should own a CRM automation strategy?

One named person, usually in revenue operations or sales operations on a growing team, or the founder directly on a very small one. Ownership split across whoever happens to build each rule is how a strategy quietly becomes an unmanaged pile of individually sensible automations.
