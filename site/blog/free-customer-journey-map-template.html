Customer journey mapping

A free customer journey map template. Built to fill in this week, not file away.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.25 July 20266 min read

This customer journey map template tracks four stages, awareness, consideration, decision and the first 90 days, against the question a customer is actually asking at each one, where they go to answer it, and what would make your business the obvious choice. Fill it in from real conversations, not assumptions, and it takes under an hour for one segment.

What the template covers. Four stages, three questions.

Most journey map templates ask you to plot touchpoints and emotions across a full customer lifecycle, which is useful once a team already has the discipline for it, but is a lot of format for a first attempt. This free customer journey map template is built to be filled in this week: four stages, awareness, consideration, decision, and the first 90 days after they buy, each tracked against three simple rows, the question in the customer's head, where they go looking for the answer, and what makes your business the obvious choice at that exact moment.

StageTheir questionWhere they lookWhat wins them over
AwarenessIs this actually a problem I need to fix?Search, a peer's recommendation, a competitor's advertContent that names the problem specifically, before pitching a solution
ConsiderationWho can actually do this, and are they credible?Website, case studies, a first callNamed results, a clear process, direct answers to their first questions
DecisionWhat happens if this goes wrong, and what am I committing to?Proposal, references, contract termsClear scope, a named point of contact, a visible way out if it does not work
First 90 daysDid I make the right call?Onboarding communication, early results, how fast issues get resolvedA visible early win, and someone who checks in before being chased

The "first 90 days" row is the one most templates leave out entirely, because they stop at the sale. It matters because a customer's honest answer to "did I make the right call" is being formed in that window whether you manage it deliberately or not, and it is one of the best predictors of whether they renew, refer anyone, or quietly start looking at a competitor.

How to use it. One segment, real conversations, under an hour.

Fill in the template from actual conversations, five to ten recent sales calls, onboarding notes or support tickets, rather than a team's best guess at what a customer thinks. McKinsey's research on customer experience has found that companies leading on customer experience grow revenue four to eight per cent faster than the wider market, and that gap comes from exactly this kind of specific, evidence-based understanding of what a customer needs at each stage, not a generic assumption applied to everyone.

Map one customer segment at a time. A business selling to both individual buyers and to procurement teams inside larger companies is answering a genuinely different question at every stage for each group, and forcing both into one map produces something too vague to act on for either. If more than one segment matters to the business, that is more than one template, not one template with extra rows.

Keep it to one page. The value of this format is that a team can look at all four stages at once during a workshop and argue about where a specific piece of evidence belongs, in "consideration" or "decision", for instance, which is usually where the most useful conversation in the room happens. For the fuller model behind this exercise, see our approach to customer journey mapping, and for a version built around the full customer relationship rather than just the path to the sale, the customer experience journey map template covers the five-stage version, including what happens after the first 90 days.

Revisit the map every two to three months in its first year, not because the format demands it but because early answers are usually wrong in small, specific ways that only show up once someone tests the assumptions against a few more real conversations. After the first year, once the questions and answers have stopped shifting much between updates, an annual refresh is normally enough, unless the business changes what it sells or who it sells to in the meantime.

A worked example. A UK accounting firm's first 90 days.

A small UK accounting firm taking on SME clients used this template for its most common client type, a founder switching accountants after outgrowing their current one. The awareness-stage question turned out not to be "do I need an accountant", which the firm had assumed, but "is switching worth the hassle right now", a genuinely different problem to address in early content and adverts.

At the decision stage, prospects were not asking about price first, they were asking what would happen to their existing filings and deadlines mid-switch, a practical worry the firm's proposal template did not address at all. Adding one paragraph that answered it directly, before price was even mentioned, was the single change that came straight out of filling in this template.

The first 90 days row exposed the real gap. Clients said yes expecting a smooth handover, then heard nothing concrete until their first VAT return was due, weeks later. The firm added one scheduled call in week two, purely to confirm what had already been done and what was coming next, no pitch, no upsell. That single low-cost change, sourced directly from the template rather than a general "improve onboarding" initiative, was what stopped a run of early client complaints about feeling ignored right after signing. For the buying-committee side of this, where more than one person is involved in the decision, mapping the buyer journey covers the stakeholders a purchase-focused map needs to account for.

None of this required a workshop facilitator, a licensed piece of software or a full afternoon out of the diary. It took one person an hour with the four-stage table above, ten client conversations already sitting in call notes and support tickets, and the discipline to write down what clients actually said rather than what the team assumed they would say. That is the entire point of a template built to be filled in this week rather than scheduled for next quarter.

Common questions.

Do I need special software to fill this template in?

No. A shared document, a whiteboard or a single slide works fine for a first attempt at one segment. Dedicated journey mapping software earns its cost once a business is maintaining several maps and updating them from live data, but it is not needed to get real value from this exercise the first time round.

What if I do not have enough real customer conversations to fill it in properly yet?

Start with whatever exists: five recent sales calls, a handful of support tickets, or notes from the last few onboarding sessions. A map built from ten real conversations beats one built from an internal guess, even if it feels thin. Add to it as more conversations happen rather than waiting for a large enough sample first.

Who should own filling this template in, marketing, sales or customer success?

Whoever is closest to the customer at each stage should contribute that row, but one person should own pulling it together and keeping it current. In most founder-led businesses that is whoever runs revenue operations or customer success, since they are the ones who see the whole path, not just one stage of it.

Is this template suitable for a B2C business, or only B2B?

Both. The four stages, awareness, consideration, decision and the first 90 days, describe any considered purchase, whether the buyer is an individual consumer or a procurement team. What changes between B2C and B2B is how many people are involved in the decision stage, not the shape of the template itself.

What should happen immediately after the template is filled in?

Pick the single row with the clearest gap between what the customer needs and what currently happens, assign it a named owner and a deadline, and fix that one thing before starting on the rest. A map that produces one shipped change is worth more than a map that produces a long list nobody actions.
