Go-to-market strategy

Go-to-market for the UAE. Built for how the market actually buys.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.25 November 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a Dubai-based GCC market entry and revenue operations consultant. More on her background at the about section.

Going to market in the UAE commercially, not legally, means getting four decisions right before you spend on acquisition: who you are positioning to and why they should trust you first, which channel actually carries that trust (direct, distributor, or a named local partner), how pricing and payment terms hold up locally, and in what order you sequence relationship-building before volume. Get the sequencing wrong and the rest rarely saves it.

The short answer. Structure the market entity question separately from the commercial one.

I'm Lauren Pearson, and I split UAE launches into two separate questions that founders often collapse into one: how you are legally set up to operate here, and how you actually go to market once you are. The first question, free zone versus mainland, banking, the local business calendar, is a real one, and if that is what you are trying to solve right now, our guide to doing business in the UAE covers it properly. This piece is about the second question: once you can legally trade, what actually gets a UAE buyer from first contact to a signed deal.

The short answer is that the UAE rewards a slower, more relationship-led early motion than most founders plan for, and punishes a go-to-market plan that is really just the home-market plan with the currency and a few words changed. The market is sophisticated and increasingly digital-first in how buyers research before they talk to anyone, but the trust that actually closes a B2B deal still tends to run through a person, a reference, or a locally reachable point of contact, more than it would in a market where cold digital outreach alone can close.

How it works in practice. Four decisions, in the order that actually matters.

Positioning: lead with credibility markers the market recognises. A generic value proposition that worked at home rarely lands unchanged. UAE B2B buyers, particularly in hospitality, government-adjacent and larger corporate accounts, weigh who else locally or regionally has used you before they weigh the feature list. If you do not have a local reference yet, your first commercial goal is getting one, even on terms that would not make sense as a standard deal elsewhere, because that one reference does more for the next ten conversations than another month of outbound.

Channel: direct, distributor, or a named local partner. This is the decision most founders get wrong by defaulting to whichever is easiest to set up rather than whichever matches how the buyer actually procures. A 2026 Atradius payment practices survey of companies trading in the UAE found that more than twice as many firms reported payment delays getting worse over the past year than reported them improving, with close to half citing their customers' own cash flow constraints as the main cause. That matters directly for channel choice: a distributor who already carries local payment-collection relationships and credit judgement can absorb some of that risk far better than a small overseas team collecting receivables from a new market cold. For a smaller, more technical buyer set with faster cycles, selling direct once you have a credible local reference can still outperform a distributor's margin take.

Pricing and terms: build in the collection risk, not just the exchange rate. Given that payment-delay environment, credit terms matter as much as headline pricing. A shorter payment term, a deposit structure, or working with a partner who already has local collection infrastructure is often worth more to your actual cash position than a slightly higher list price with open terms.

Sequencing: relationship and reference work before volume. The UAE's digital buying research is real and growing, UAE businesses have been reported moving quickly toward AI-assisted search and digital-first research ahead of a purchase, but that research stage still tends to end in a human conversation before a B2B deal closes, especially above a certain deal size. Plan your first ninety days around securing one or two credible references and the relationships that got you there, not around acquisition volume. Volume that arrives before that groundwork is done usually converts poorly and burns the credibility you need for the second wave of outreach.

These four decisions do not land the same way across every sector. A hospitality technology vendor is usually selling into a buyer group that already moves through regional trade events, operator networks and word of mouth between competing properties, so channel and reference work can often ride on relationships that already exist in that world. A B2B software vendor with no hospitality-style network to plug into has to build that trust from a colder start, which is exactly why the first reference matters disproportionately more for that kind of team. Treating both as the same "UAE go-to-market" plan is one of the more expensive assumptions a founder can make early on.

What good looks like. A small, named win beats a wide, generic launch.

Picture a B2B software team entering the UAE with a product that had performed well in Europe. The instinct was to run the same paid-acquisition and content motion that worked at home, translated rather than rebuilt. It produced leads and almost no closed deals in the first quarter, because none of those leads had any reason to trust an unfamiliar overseas vendor over a locally known alternative they were already evaluating. The team changed course: one named local hospitality group was offered a pilot on terms that would not have scaled as a standard deal, specifically to get a UAE-based reference in place. That single relationship, once it converted, became the credibility the next round of direct outreach had been missing, and the deals that followed closed faster and with far less persuasion.

That is what good UAE go-to-market usually looks like in its first two quarters: narrow, reference-led, and deliberately slower than a founder's instinct wants it to be, in exchange for a second wave of sales that moves considerably faster once the first reference exists.

It also pays to plan for how many people actually sign off on a UAE B2B purchase once you get past the smallest companies. A procurement process for a mid-size or larger UAE organisation commonly involves more named stakeholders than the equivalent deal would at home, a technical evaluator, a finance approver, and often someone senior whose informal buy-in matters as much as any formal sign-off. Mapping that stakeholder group early, rather than discovering it deal by deal, shortens the sales cycle considerably, because the conversations that would otherwise happen in sequence, one surprise stakeholder at a time, can instead happen in parallel once you know who needs to be in the room.

Pitfalls to avoid. The mistakes that cost the most time.

The single biggest pitfall is treating the UAE as one market. A Dubai-based hospitality buyer, an Abu Dhabi government-adjacent buyer and a Sharjah-based SME behave differently enough that a single undifferentiated pitch tends to underperform with all three rather than serve any of them well; our guide to expanding into the Middle East without a local team covers the related question of how to stay commercially present without overbuilding your local footprint too early, which is worth reading alongside this if you are still deciding how much local presence you actually need.

The second is under-planning for the relationship-building timeline and over-planning the acquisition funnel, building paid campaigns and content calendars before you have a single local person who can open a door for you. The third is ignoring the payment-terms risk above until it shows up as a cash-flow problem months into the first contracts, rather than pricing and structuring for it from the first deal. A fourth, quieter pitfall is picking a channel partner for their size or brand recognition rather than for how closely their existing buyer relationships match yours; a large distributor with no real presence in your specific sector will often move slower than a smaller, more focused partner who already calls on the exact accounts you need. Each of these is fixable, but all four are considerably cheaper to plan around up front than to unwind once a launch is already underway and the wrong partner or the wrong pricing structure is already in a signed contract.

One more worth naming directly: assuming the sale closes the same way it did back home. A founder used to a largely self-serve or inbound motion elsewhere sometimes expects the UAE to eventually convert the same way once brand awareness builds. In practice, even strong inbound interest in the UAE tends to still want a call, a reference, and in many cases an in-person or video introduction before signing, regardless of deal size or how well-produced the website is. Budgeting the sales motion, and the time of whoever is doing those calls, into the plan from day one avoids the common surprise of healthy website traffic that never quite turns into signed revenue.

None of this means the UAE is a slow market overall, it is a fast-moving one by most other measures, trade volumes through Dubai alone have continued to grow year on year and the appetite for new technology and new suppliers is genuinely high. It means the speed shows up later in the process than founders expect, concentrated in the second and third deals rather than the first, once the credibility work the market asks for has actually been done.

Common questions.

What is different about go to market in the UAE compared with the UK or US?

Relationships carry more of the early trust-building work before a buyer will take a commercial conversation seriously, procurement in larger organisations tends to involve more named stakeholders than a comparable deal elsewhere, and a locally reachable point of contact matters more than it would in a market where cold digital outreach alone can close a deal.

Do I need a local entity to go to market in the UAE?

Not to start testing the market commercially. A free zone entity, a local distributor, or an appointed commercial agent can each get you in front of real buyers before you commit to a full local operation. The legal and entity question is separate from the go-to-market question, and worth answering on its own rather than defaulting to whichever structure is easiest to set up first.

Should I sell direct or through a distributor in the UAE?

It depends on how much of the buying decision happens through a relationship a distributor already owns. For sectors where procurement runs through established local networks, hospitality and government-adjacent buyers among them, a partner with that relationship often outperforms a direct motion that has to build trust from nothing. For a smaller, more technical buyer set, direct can work well once you have one credible local reference.

How long does it take to see traction going to market in the UAE?

Longer than most founders plan for in the first quarter, and meaningfully faster after the first reference customer is in place. The early months are mostly relationship and credibility work rather than volume, and trying to force volume before that groundwork is done tends to produce a pipeline that looks busy and converts poorly.

What is the most common go-to-market mistake in the UAE?

Running the same channel mix and messaging that worked at home with only the currency and a few words changed. The UAE is not one market either, a Dubai-based hospitality buyer and an Abu Dhabi government-adjacent buyer behave quite differently, and treating the whole country as a single homogeneous segment is where most generic launches lose momentum.
