Go-to-market strategy

Go-to-market metrics that matter. Fewer numbers, watched more closely.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.21 October 20268 min read

The go-to-market metrics worth tracking at launch are CAC payback period, activation rate within a defined early window, and pipeline velocity broken down by channel, not blended together. Track all three from day one, per channel, and watch the trend over several months rather than chasing a single benchmark number too early.

The short answer. Three numbers, tracked per channel, from day one.

Go-to-market metrics get complicated fast if you let them, because almost every function wants its own dashboard. In practice, three numbers carry most of the weight at launch. CAC payback period tells you how many months it takes a new customer to repay what it cost to acquire them, your efficiency number. Activation rate, the share of new customers who reach a defined point of real product use within an early window, tells you whether the people you're acquiring are actually getting value, your quality number. Pipeline velocity by channel, how fast leads from each specific channel move into real pipeline, tells you which channel is worth scaling and which is producing volume that never converts, your allocation number.

The Aleph x Benchmarkit 2026 B2B SaaS benchmarks report, covering full-year 2025 data from 342 B2B SaaS and AI-native software companies, put the median CAC payback period at 16 months, improved from 18 months the year before, with the SaaS Magic Number, a measure of growth efficiency, rising to a median of 1.37 across the same set. Those are useful reference points, not targets to hit on day one of a launch; a brand-new go-to-market motion rarely matches an established median in its first few months, and chasing the benchmark too early usually means cutting spend on a channel before it's had time to mature.

These three aren't the only numbers a go-to-market team could track, and a mature RevOps function will eventually want more: win rate by segment, sales cycle length, net revenue retention once customers have been live long enough to measure it. But at launch, before enough volume exists to make a longer list statistically meaningful, adding more numbers mostly adds noise. The discipline is choosing three you can genuinely watch every week, not assembling a dashboard that looks complete but gets opened once a month.

Why these three specifically. What each one would miss on its own.

CAC payback on its own tells you nothing about whether the customers you're acquiring stick around long enough to be worth keeping; a channel can pay back quickly and still churn badly. Activation rate on its own tells you nothing about cost; a channel can activate customers beautifully while costing far more than the revenue it eventually returns. Pipeline velocity on its own tells you nothing about quality; a channel can move leads into pipeline fast precisely because the deal criteria are loose enough to let almost anything through. Each number corrects a blind spot in the other two, which is the real reason to track exactly these three together rather than picking a single favourite.

This matters more at launch than later, because a new go-to-market motion is usually working with thin data and strong opinions. Whoever is closest to the paid channel will advocate for pipeline velocity as the headline number; whoever is closest to product will advocate for activation; whoever is closest to finance will advocate for CAC payback. Agreeing upfront that all three matter, and that no single function's preferred metric gets to be the only one on the board, heads off a lot of the internal disagreement that otherwise surfaces three months in, once the launch is underway and the pressure to show a good number has already started.

How it works in practice. Per channel, before blended.

The mistake I see most often is tracking these three numbers in aggregate from the start. A blended CAC payback of fourteen months looks perfectly healthy until you split it by channel and find one channel paying back in six months while another never pays back at all, subsidised invisibly by the first. Build the tracking per channel from day one, even if the volume in any single channel is small enough that the numbers feel noisy; noisy-but-separated data tells you more than smooth-but-blended data at this stage.

Activation rate needs the same per-channel discipline, plus a defined window that doesn't move. Pick a specific action, a second meaningful session, a key feature used, a first real outcome delivered, and a specific timeframe, say fourteen days, and hold both constant across cohorts. Changing the definition partway through a launch to make the number look better than a prior cohort defeats the entire point of tracking it. Pipeline velocity works best measured as time from first contact to a qualified stage, by channel, rather than as a single average across the whole funnel. A channel that's slow to produce a first contact but fast to qualify once it does is a very different problem from a channel that produces fast first contact and then stalls. Blending the two numbers into one velocity figure hides which half of the funnel actually needs attention, and most teams end up fixing the wrong half as a result.

What good looks like. Trend, not snapshot.

A healthy go-to-market motion shows CAC payback shortening and activation rate holding steady or improving as volume grows, not a single good month surrounded by noise. Watch the trend across at least a full quarter before drawing a conclusion about any one channel, and resist the temptation to kill a channel after one disappointing month if the sample size is still small. A useful habit at this stage is a monthly one-page review: three numbers, per channel, with last month's figure next to this month's, and a short note on anything that moved more than you'd expect. It doesn't need a dedicated analytics tool in the first few months; a shared spreadsheet pulled from the CRM and ad platforms is enough, and building the habit of reviewing it matters more than the sophistication of the tool doing the pulling. Teams that wait for the "proper" dashboard before starting the review tend to lose the first quarter of data they'll later wish they had.

Pitfalls to avoid. Volume without retention, and the channel that looks fast.

A hospitality technology client I worked with during an early market launch found one paid channel that was producing leads and converting into pipeline faster than any other channel they ran, by a wide margin on pipeline velocity alone. The instinct was to shift budget towards it immediately. Before doing that, we checked ninety-day retention on the customers that channel had actually closed, and found it was meaningfully worse than the slower, more expensive channel it was about to replace: the fast channel was bringing in a buyer who signed quickly and churned just as quickly. This is an illustrative pattern, not a single named client result, but the decision rule holds broadly: never scale a channel on pipeline velocity or CAC payback alone before checking what happens to the customers it brings in, sixty or ninety days after the deal closes.

The second common pitfall is treating go-to-market metrics as a launch-week exercise rather than an ongoing discipline. Teams often build the dashboard for the launch announcement and stop maintaining it once the initial excitement fades, right around the point where the data would start being genuinely useful for deciding what to do next. Name one owner for the three core numbers before launch, not after, and keep the per-channel view running for at least two full quarters before making a permanent allocation decision.

A third, quieter pitfall is comparing a new channel's early numbers to an established channel's mature ones. A channel that's been running for two years has had time to refine targeting, messaging and qualification; a channel three weeks into launch hasn't. Judging the new channel against the old one's current numbers, rather than against what the old one looked like in its own first month, usually kills promising channels too early and keeps mediocre established ones running on reputation alone.

Getting started. Start smaller than the dashboard you're imagining.

Before building an elaborate go-to-market dashboard, confirm you can answer three questions from memory, for each channel: what's the CAC payback, what's the activation rate, and how fast is pipeline actually moving. If you can't answer all three for every channel you're running, that's the gap worth closing first, before adding more channels or more spend on top of numbers nobody's watching closely enough. Our go-to-market strategy work usually starts exactly there, not with a bigger launch plan, but with a smaller, better-watched set of numbers underneath the one that's already running.

If you're expanding an existing go-to-market motion into a new market rather than launching from scratch, the same three numbers still apply, but expect the early benchmarks to look worse before they look better; unfamiliar buyer behaviour and an unproven local message both take a few months to settle. Our work with hospitality technology firms entering new regions follows the same pattern: resist comparing month one in a new market against a mature number from the home market, and give the new motion the same two-quarter runway you'd give any other channel before deciding whether it's working.

Common questions.

What are the most important go-to-market metrics to track at launch?

CAC payback period, activation rate within the first meaningful window, and pipeline velocity broken down by channel. These three together show whether you're acquiring customers efficiently, whether they're actually getting value, and which channel is producing pipeline fast enough to matter, rather than just producing leads.

How soon after launch should we start tracking go-to-market metrics?

From day one, even if the numbers are small and noisy. Waiting for enough volume to feel statistically meaningful means losing the early weeks of channel and messaging data that's often the most honest signal you'll get, before paid spend or sales process changes start masking the underlying pattern.

What's a good CAC payback period for a go-to-market launch?

The Aleph x Benchmarkit 2026 B2B SaaS benchmarks report put the median CAC payback period at 16 months for 2025 full-year data, improved from 18 months in 2024, with top-quartile companies well under that. A new launch won't usually match the median immediately; the useful comparison is your own trend over several months, not the benchmark in isolation.

Should go-to-market metrics differ by channel?

Yes, track them per channel before you track them in aggregate. A blended CAC payback or activation rate can look healthy while hiding one channel that's performing badly and another that's carrying it, and you can't fix or scale either one if they're hidden inside a single combined number.
