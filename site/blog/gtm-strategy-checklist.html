Go-to-market strategy

A go-to-market strategy checklist. Five sections, done in order.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.4 November 20267 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a go-to-market and RevOps consultant for founder-led teams. More on her background at the about section.

A go-to-market strategy checklist should confirm five decisions in order: who you're selling to, why they'd choose you over the alternative, what you charge and how, which channel will actually reach them, and what has to be ready, sales, support and tracking, before the first customer arrives. Doing the steps out of order is the most common way a launch underperforms.

What the checklist covers. Five decisions, not fifty tasks.

A go to market checklist gets treated, too often, as a project plan: a long list of tasks with owners and dates. That's launch planning, and it matters, but it's a different document from this one. A go-to-market strategy checklist exists to confirm that five specific decisions were actually made, deliberately, before any of those tasks start, because a beautifully executed launch built on the wrong decision still fails.

SectionThe decision it confirms
Ideal customer profileWho you're selling to, specifically enough to exclude most of the market, not just describe it
PositioningWhy that customer chooses you over the next-best alternative they'd actually consider
Pricing and packagingWhat you charge, how it's structured, and whether that structure matches how the customer buys
Channel and motionWhether you're selling, self-serve or product-led, and which channel actually reaches this ICP at this stage
Launch readinessWhether sales, support and tracking can handle a customer arriving tomorrow, not in a month

The order matters more than the content of any single row. Pricing decided before positioning tends to anchor on cost or competitor rates rather than the value the customer actually gets, and channel decided before the ICP is specific enough almost always defaults to whatever channel the founder is personally comfortable with, rather than the one the buyer actually uses. Our guide to the components of a go-to-market strategy goes deeper on each of these five pieces individually; this checklist is about sequencing them and confirming each one is genuinely decided, not assumed.

How to use it. One pass, then a second pass before launch.

Run the checklist twice. The first pass happens when the strategy is being set, ideally four to six weeks before launch, and the goal is simply to force every decision into writing: a one-paragraph ICP, a one-sentence positioning statement, a pricing table, a named primary channel, and a list of who owns readiness in sales, support and tracking. Vague answers at this stage ("mid-market companies", "better than the competition") are a sign the decision hasn't actually been made yet, just gestured at.

The second pass happens in the week before launch, and it's a readiness check rather than a strategy check: has sales actually been briefed on the final positioning, not an earlier draft of it; does support have answers to the three objections most likely to come up; is the dashboard that will measure success built and tested with real data, not a mock-up. Teams that only run the checklist once, at the strategy stage, routinely discover on launch day that the thing agreed on paper six weeks earlier never made it into the team actually facing the customer.

A worked example. Where the order actually changed the outcome.

A B2B SaaS founder I worked with, illustrative rather than a named client, had already built a pricing page and a launch date before we sat down. The pricing was tiered by number of seats, a structure borrowed from a competitor, and the positioning draft led with "all-in-one platform". Running the checklist in order surfaced the problem immediately: the actual ICP, once we wrote it down specifically rather than gesturing at "small businesses", was operations teams at companies with 20 to 80 staff who were buying to replace three disconnected spreadsheets, not to add a platform. Seat-based pricing punished exactly the behaviour we wanted to encourage, getting every operations person using the tool, and "all-in-one platform" meant nothing to a buyer whose actual problem was three specific spreadsheets.

The fix wasn't a bigger launch budget or a longer runway; it was redoing two rows of the checklist, pricing and positioning, before touching the channel plan that was already built. The positioning became a direct claim about replacing those three spreadsheets, and pricing moved to a flat team price that encouraged full adoption rather than per-seat gatekeeping. The channel plan, built for the wrong positioning, barely needed to change once the two decisions underneath it were corrected. The decision rule that follows from it: if a launch isn't landing, check the ICP and positioning rows before touching the channel or the budget, because a channel problem is usually a positioning problem wearing a different costume.

Common mistakes with a go-to-market checklist. Treating it as a formality.

  • Writing the ICP from who you'd like to sell to, not who already buys. A checklist ICP row filled in with aspiration rather than evidence from actual early customers sends the rest of the plan chasing the wrong buyer.
  • Setting pricing before positioning is settled. Pricing anchored to a competitor's rate card rather than to the value your positioning actually claims almost always undersells or oversells the offer.
  • Picking a channel because it's familiar, not because the ICP is there. A founder comfortable with content and SEO will default to it even for a buyer who makes decisions through referral or outbound, because the channel row gets filled in from the founder's comfort rather than the buyer's behaviour.
  • Treating the readiness row as administrative. Sales enablement and support readiness get scheduled last and compressed first when the launch date slips, which is exactly backwards: readiness is what the customer actually experiences.

None of these five sections needs to be complicated to be right. What they need is to be decided in order, written down specifically enough that a stranger could act on them, and checked a second time the week before anyone outside the team sees the launch. That discipline, more than any single tactic on the list, is what separates a go-to-market plan that was genuinely built from one that was assembled from habit. Where the checklist keeps surfacing the same stuck row launch after launch, that's usually less a go-to-market problem than a growth consulting conversation about the business underneath it.

Common questions.

What should a go-to-market strategy checklist actually cover?

Five things, in order: who you're selling to (ICP), why they'd choose you (positioning), what you charge and how (pricing and packaging), how you'll reach them (channel and motion), and what has to be ready on the day you launch (sales, support and tracking). Skipping the order, especially pricing before positioning, is the most common mistake.

Is a go-to-market checklist different from a go-to-market strategy?

Yes. The strategy is the set of decisions, who you're targeting, how you'll position and price, which motion you'll run. The checklist is the operational tool that confirms each of those decisions was actually made, and made in the right order, before you spend money on launch activity.

How far in advance should the checklist be completed before launch?

Four to six weeks for most founder-led launches, enough time to validate positioning with a handful of real prospect conversations and brief sales or support before the first customer arrives, but not so far out that the market or product shifts under the plan before it ships.

What's the single most skipped item on a go-to-market checklist?

Defining what success looks like for the first 90 days before launch, not after it. Teams routinely agree on positioning and channel, launch, and only then argue about which number actually proves it worked, by which point the data needed to answer that question properly often hasn't been tracked from day one.

Do I need a different checklist for a new product versus entering a new market?

The core five sections are the same, but a new-market entry adds a layer: local buyer behaviour, competitive set and regulatory or payment norms specific to that market. Our market entry checklist for hospitality technology firms covers that layer in more depth for a Gulf launch specifically.
