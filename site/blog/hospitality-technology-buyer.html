Hospitality tech & Middle East expansion

The hospitality technology buyer. It's rarely one person.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.26 October 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a hospitality-tech market entry and RevOps consultant. More on her background at the about section.

The hospitality technology buyer is usually a small group, not one decision-maker: an operations or GM sponsor who owns the problem, an IT or corporate lead who owns integration and security, and finance or procurement who owns the contract. Selling into hospitality means answering all three, roughly in that order, not pitching features to whoever first took the call.

The short answer. Three roles, one deal.

Hospitality tech vendors who pitch as if there's a single "buyer" on the other end of the call usually lose time they don't notice they're losing. In practice, a purchase moves through three roles. The sponsor, often a general manager, operations director or department head (F&B, revenue, front office), is the person who feels the problem and champions the fix internally. The technical gatekeeper, usually IT or a corporate systems lead, owns whether the tool can actually connect to the property management system, the point of sale or the central reservations platform without creating a security gap. Finance or procurement owns the contract itself: term length, currency, payment schedule and, for groups, whether the spend sits at property or corporate level.

A 2026 Hotel Technology Outlook report from New York University's Jonathan Tisch hospitality school, produced with Stayntouch and IDeaS and released in November 2025 from more than 300 hotel professionals across roles and property types, found a renewed shift toward best-of-breed systems built around a core property management platform, rather than single-vendor all-in-one suites. That shift matters here because it hands the technical gatekeeper more influence than a few years ago: when hoteliers are choosing to stitch systems together rather than buy one bundle, integration fit stops being a late-stage technical check and becomes a reason deals get shortlisted or dropped early.

  • The sponsor. A GM, operations director or department head who feels the problem day to day and champions the fix. They can sell internally, but rarely hold final sign-off alone once a property sits inside a group.
  • The technical gatekeeper. IT or a corporate systems lead who checks integration with the property management, point of sale or revenue system, and data handling. Increasingly the role with the earliest veto, not the latest.
  • Finance or procurement. Owns the contract itself, term length, currency and whether the spend sits at property or corporate level. Usually the last signature, and in a group, rarely the same person who championed the deal.

How it works in practice. Property first, corporate reviews.

For a single independent property, the owner-operator or GM genuinely can decide alone, and the sales cycle can move in weeks. For a group of two or more properties, the pattern changes. A GM or department head at one property usually initiates, often after seeing a problem a peer property has already solved, then the deal is referred upward. Corporate IT reviews data handling and integration with whatever central systems the group already runs. A regional operations lead or VP checks whether the tool fits how the brand standard works across properties, not just the one that asked for it. Only once those two have signed off does the deal reach finance for contract terms, and in a multi-property group that's frequently a different signature than the one who championed it.

Budget ownership follows the same split and is worth checking early rather than assuming. A tool that solves a single property's problem is often funded from that property's own operating budget, which a GM can approve with comparatively little friction. A tool a group wants standardised across every property usually becomes a corporate capital or technology budget line instead, reviewed on an annual cycle rather than approved on demand, which changes the realistic timeline for a deal from weeks to a quarter or more. Asking which budget a purchase would come from, early, tends to surface this distinction faster than asking about authority directly.

The Middle East difference. Ownership, relationships, and who holds the real veto.

The three roles above hold across markets, but the Middle East changes who sits above them. A meaningful share of GCC hotel and restaurant groups operate under family-owned holding companies, so there is often a fourth, quieter party: an ownership representative or board-level sponsor who can override a GM's enthusiasm or a corporate IT sign-off with a single conversation, and who rarely appears on the vendor's call sheet until late. Deals here also tend to open through a warm introduction, an existing reference property, or a regional partner, rather than inbound demand generation, and that isn't a cultural footnote, it changes which of the three roles you're able to reach first. The pace of the cycle follows from the same structure. Where a Western chain might run a formal RFP with a fixed timeline, a GCC ownership group more often decides through a series of in-person meetings and a trusted introduction, where trust in the relationship carries as much weight as the feature comparison. Vendors used to a fast, document-driven process sometimes read that slower pace as disinterest, when it's usually the opposite: the deal is moving, just through a different sequence than the one their sales playbook assumes.

Pitfalls to avoid. Pitching the person who answered the phone.

A hospitality SaaS company I worked with, illustrative rather than a named result, spent nearly four months in what looked like a closing conversation with an enthusiastic GM at a mid-size property group. The GM genuinely wanted the tool and had said so repeatedly. What nobody had done was loop in corporate IT until the contract was drafted, at which point IT raised a legitimate integration concern with the group's existing PMS that could have been answered in week one. The deal didn't die; it lost most of a quarter to a question that should have been asked at the first call, not the last one. The decision rule that follows: ask who else needs to say yes before you build the champion's enthusiasm into your forecast, not after.

A second, quieter pitfall is assuming the same buying structure holds for every property type. A boutique independent hotel, a branded mid-scale property, and a multi-unit restaurant group each distribute the three roles differently, and a pitch tuned for one often lands flat with another.

Getting started. Map the buyer before you build the deck.

Before a first serious call, it's worth asking directly:

  • Who owns the budget line this purchase would come from, property or corporate?
  • Who would need to approve a change to the systems this would connect with?
  • Does this property sit inside a group with a corporate or regional review step?

Those three questions, asked plainly and early, usually surface the real buyer faster than any amount of discovery on the champion's own pain points. Common questions.

Who decides on hospitality technology purchases?

Usually three roles acting together, not one buyer: an operations or general manager sponsor who owns the problem the tool solves, an IT or corporate technology lead who owns integration and data security, and finance or procurement who owns the contract. A vendor that wins over only one of the three usually stalls rather than closes.

Is the hospitality technology buyer different in the Middle East?

The roles are the same but the pace and the trust-building differ. Many GCC hotel and restaurant groups sit under family-owned holding companies, so an ownership representative often sits above the GM sponsor with real veto power, and a first meeting usually needs a warm introduction or a reference property rather than inbound outreach alone.

Does the general manager or the corporate office decide for hotel groups?

For a single independent property, the GM or owner-operator can usually decide alone. For a group of two or more properties, the GM typically sponsors and champions the tool, but corporate IT and often a regional operations lead hold a review and veto, especially over integration with the central property management or revenue system.

What is the biggest mistake hospitality tech vendors make when selling?

Treating the person who takes the first call as the whole buyer. A GM or department head can be genuinely enthusiastic and still have no authority to sign, and if IT and finance are brought in only at contract stage, integration or budget objections that could have been resolved early can kill a deal that looked closed.
