Hospitality Tech & Middle East Expansion

Hotel technology in the GCC. What's actually changing, and what to check before buying.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.30 October 20267 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a Dubai-based consultant who helps hospitality technology companies expand into the GCC. More on her background at the about section.

Hotel technology in the GCC means UAE, Saudi and Qatar properties moving from older, on-premise systems to cloud-hosted property management, channel management and guest messaging platforms, driven by rapid hotel-count growth and rising guest expectations. For a vendor, the practical question is which system a given property is replacing right now.

The short answer on hotel technology in the GCC. A fast-growing market still catching up its systems.

The GCC Statistical Centre's 2026 bulletin, "Gulf Tourism in the Era of Smart Transformation," put the number of hotel establishments across the GCC at around 12,400 in 2025, up 4.8% on 2024, with regional tourism revenue reaching roughly $131.9 billion, up 9.7% on the year before. That pace of growth is itself a driver of the technology shift: a portfolio adding properties every year cannot keep configuring each new one on the same legacy system its flagship property has run for a decade, and most groups I talk to in the region reach that conclusion somewhere around their third or fourth new property.

The direction is consistent across the region even where hard numbers are scarce. A 2026 interview with Jimmy Joseph, Director of CADD Emirates, published on Hospitality Net, described UAE and Saudi hotels actively moving off legacy hardware toward cloud-based, integrated systems as portfolios expand and guest expectations rise. That matches what I see on the ground: branded international chains migrated years ago, and the activity now is concentrated in independent and regional groups catching up, often triggered by a new property opening rather than a decision to replace what already works.

The commercial point for anyone selling into this market is less "the GCC is growing" and more specific: growth at this pace creates a steady supply of greenfield decisions, a new property with no incumbent system to dislodge, rather than the harder sell of replacing something that already works tolerably well. A vendor's real opportunity in the region tends to sit with the next property a group opens, not with convincing an existing one to rip out a system it has already paid for and trained staff on. That is a different sales motion to the one most Western hospitality tech companies are used to running, and it rewards a vendor who tracks which groups are actively expanding rather than one who simply pitches every property in the market on the same replacement cycle.

How it works in practice. The systems actually changing, by category.

CategoryWhat's changingWhy it matters for a vendor
Property management (PMS)On-premise servers giving way to cloud-hosted platforms, usually at the point a group opens a new propertyA closed, legacy PMS with no real API blocks every other integration on this list
Channel management & distributionSyncing availability and rates across OTAs, the direct booking engine and, increasingly, regional booking platforms alongside the global onesA channel manager that only covers Booking.com and Expedia misses the OTAs guests in this market actually use
Guest messagingMoving from email and SMS to WhatsApp as the primary channel for pre-arrival and in-stay contactA messaging tool without native WhatsApp support starts at a real disadvantage in this region specifically
Revenue managementRule-based pricing giving way to AI-assisted dynamic pricing tied to demand signalsNeeds clean, integrated PMS and channel data to work; bolted onto a legacy system, it under-performs

Of these four, guest messaging is the one outside vendors most often get wrong, because it is the one where the difference from a home market is behavioural rather than technical. Guests and hotel staff across the UAE and wider GCC default to WhatsApp for day-to-day communication ahead of email, in a way that is more pronounced than in most European or US markets. A product built around email-first guest communication, however well it performs elsewhere, tends to see visibly lower engagement here, not because the technology is worse but because it is asking guests to use a channel they would not otherwise choose.

Channel management carries a similar regional wrinkle. A distribution setup built for a European or US property typically assumes two or three OTAs carry most of the volume, and that assumption travels badly into a market where regional and pan-Arab booking platforms sit alongside the global names in a property's actual booking mix. A channel manager that only maps to Booking.com and Expedia will balance rates and availability correctly for part of the property's demand and simply miss the rest, which shows up later as overbooking or rate parity issues that look like a technical bug but are actually a coverage gap.

Revenue management tools depend more than the other three on what is feeding them. An AI-assisted pricing engine bolted onto a legacy PMS with patchy, delayed data will produce pricing recommendations built on stale occupancy figures, which is a common reason a revenue management rollout under-delivers in its first year even when the tool itself is sound. The sequencing matters: PMS and channel data need to be clean and current before a revenue tool is worth adding on top.

What good looks like. Signs a system or vendor is set up properly for this market.

  • An open API on the PMS side, so a channel manager, revenue tool or guest messaging platform can actually connect to it, rather than a closed legacy system that only its own vendor can extend. This single check determines whether everything else on this list is even possible.
  • A channel manager covering the OTAs this market actually uses, not just the two or three global names that dominate a European or US distribution mix, and reconciled against the property's own booking data rather than assumed from a vendor's standard integration list.
  • WhatsApp built in as a first-class channel, not an integration bolted on after launch once a regional sales team asks why engagement is lower than expected. The products that get this right design the WhatsApp flow first and treat email as the fallback.
  • Arabic-language support alongside English, covering guest-facing messaging and, where the buyer is a hotel group rather than an individual property, the sales and onboarding process too. A product that only localises the guest-facing layer and leaves the buyer-facing side in English misses half of why local support matters.

These four show up in roughly that order of importance when I review a vendor's readiness for this market. A product can survive a weak channel manager integration longer than it can survive a closed PMS it genuinely cannot connect to, which is why that check belongs first in any technical evaluation, before pricing or contract terms are even discussed.

Where this goes wrong in practice. The same product, the same pitch, a market that does not behave the same way.

A hospitality technology company I worked with, illustrative rather than a named result, had a guest messaging product that performed well across its existing European client base and assumed the same email-first positioning and rollout process would translate once it expanded into the UAE. Adoption among the first few GCC properties it signed stayed well below what the same onboarding process produced elsewhere, and the gap was not obvious from the product itself; demos went well, and the hotels liked what they saw. The difference showed up in actual guest engagement after go-live: email open rates for pre-arrival messages ran far behind what the company's European properties saw, while the same hotels' own front-desk WhatsApp numbers were fielding a steady stream of guest questions the product was never asked to handle. Rebuilding the guest-facing flow around WhatsApp as the primary channel, with email as a fallback rather than the default, closed most of that gap within two property launches. The product had not needed rebuilding. The assumption about which channel guests would actually use had.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where vendors trip up entering this market.

  • Treating "the GCC" as one market with one buyer profile. The UAE, Saudi Arabia and Qatar differ meaningfully in buyer structure and decision speed, a point covered in more depth in our guide to the GCC hospitality market, and a technology roadmap built for one rarely transfers cleanly to the others without adjustment.
  • Assuming a closed legacy PMS can be worked around rather than replaced. If the property's existing system has no real API, no amount of clever integration on the vendor's side will make the connection reliable, and the honest answer is sometimes that the PMS itself needs to change before the rest of the stack can.
  • Leading with email-first guest communication by default. It is the single most common mismatch between a product built for a Western market and the way guests in this region actually want to be reached, and it is usually invisible until engagement data from the first few live properties comes in lower than the sales team expected.
  • Underestimating the regional OTA and payment mix. A channel manager or payment integration that only covers the two or three largest global names will miss booking volume that matters more here than a vendor's home market experience suggests, and the gap tends to surface as a rate parity or overbooking complaint rather than an obvious technical failure.

None of this requires a different product built from scratch. It requires checking, before entering a new property's stack, which system is already in place, whether it can actually be connected to, and whether the guest-facing channel the product defaults to is the one guests in that specific market will actually use. The vendors who do well here tend to be the ones who run that short checklist before the first demo, not after the first underwhelming set of adoption numbers from a live property.

Common questions.

What does hotel technology in the GCC actually mean?

It means the systems UAE, Saudi and Qatar properties run their operations on, chiefly the property management system, channel management and distribution tools, guest messaging, and revenue management software, and the shift many of these properties are making from older, on-premise versions of each to cloud-hosted ones.

How big is the GCC hotel market right now?

The GCC Statistical Centre's 2026 bulletin put the number of hotel establishments across the GCC at about 12,400 in 2025, up 4.8% on 2024, with regional tourism revenue reaching roughly $131.9 billion, up 9.7% on the previous year. Growth at that pace is itself a reason older, on-premise systems are being replaced faster than in slower-growing markets.

Is WhatsApp actually important for hotel technology in this region?

Yes, more than most vendors entering the market expect. Guests and staff across the UAE and wider GCC default to WhatsApp for day-to-day communication ahead of email or SMS, and a guest messaging or booking tool that treats WhatsApp as an afterthought rather than a first-class channel tends to see noticeably lower guest engagement in this market than it does elsewhere.

Do GCC hotels need technology built differently to Europe or the US?

Not rebuilt from scratch, but adapted in specific ways: Arabic-language support, regional OTA and payment integrations alongside the global ones, and a channel manager that actually covers the booking sources guests in this market use, rather than assuming the same mix of OTAs that dominates in a European or US market.

Are GCC hotels still using legacy, on-premise PMS systems?

Many are, particularly outside the branded international chains, though that is changing. Industry commentary from the region, including a 2026 interview with CADD Emirates, points to UAE and Saudi properties actively moving off legacy hardware toward cloud-based systems as portfolios expand and guest expectations rise.

What should a vendor check before entering the GCC hotel technology market?

Which system the target property is actually replacing, whether its PMS has an open API or a closed one that blocks integration, which OTAs and payment methods matter in that specific market, and whether the sales and support process can run in Arabic and English with WhatsApp as a primary channel.
