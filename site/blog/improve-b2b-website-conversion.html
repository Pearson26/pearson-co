Conversion & funnel optimisation

How to improve B2B website conversion. A guide for longer sales cycles.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.5 August 20268 min read

You improve b2b website conversion by designing for a buying committee rather than a single buyer: replace one hard CTA with a graduated path, put trust signals enterprise buyers actually check next to the form, decide gated versus ungated deliberately, and match CTA language to where a prospect sits in a longer sales cycle.

I'm Lauren Pearson, and most of the conversion advice written for websites assumes a single visitor clicking "buy now" in one sitting. That is not how B2B works. Your visitor today might be the person who found you, but rarely the only person who needs convincing, and the ask is a demo, a consultation, or a proposal request rather than a purchase. This guide is built around that difference, not around ecommerce-style tactics dressed up for a B2B audience.

The short answer. Design for a committee, not a click.

B2B buying decisions run through multiple stakeholders: a technical evaluator checking whether the thing actually works, a budget holder checking the numbers, sometimes procurement checking terms, and often an end user who will actually have to live with the tool day to day. Your website has to give each of those roles a reason to say yes, or at least a reason not to say no, even though only one of them is looking at the page right now.

That reshapes every conversion decision on the page. A single, high-commitment CTA works fine for a visitor who is ready to buy today. For everyone else, and in most B2B funnels that is the clear majority, the page needs a lower-commitment next step that still moves the relationship forward: a short assessment, a specific guide, a comparison. Enterprise trust signals need to sit close to the point of decision, not buried in a testimonials page nobody clicks through to. And whether a piece of content sits behind a form or not should be a deliberate choice about what that specific asset is for, not a blanket policy applied to everything you publish.

None of this replaces the fundamentals of good conversion and funnel optimisation: clear copy, fast pages, a form that does not ask for more than it needs. It sits on top of them. The tactics below are the layer specific to a B2B sales cycle, and they are the ones I see get skipped most often because they were written for a different kind of buyer.

Step by step. Five changes that move a B2B page.

Step 1: map the page to the buying committee, not one buyer

Before touching any copy, list the roles who realistically touch this decision. For a mid-market SaaS or services purchase, that is usually three or four people: a technical or operational evaluator, a budget holder, an end user, and occasionally procurement. Read the page again with each of those roles in mind and ask whether it gives them anything. If the entire page is written to reassure a technical evaluator and says nothing a budget holder could use to justify the spend to their own manager, you have a gap that a single, well-optimised CTA will not fix.

Step 2: replace the single hard CTA with a graduated path

Keep "book a demo" or "request a consultation" as the primary action, but add a genuinely useful lower-commitment option next to it, aimed at the visitor who is not ready for that conversation yet. A short self-assessment, a specific comparison guide, or a calculator relevant to the page topic all work. The point is not to dilute the primary CTA, it is to stop losing the majority of visitors who would take a smaller step today but will not take a large one.

Step 3: put enterprise trust signals directly next to the CTA

Client logos and a specific case study figure placed immediately around the form convert better than the same evidence three sections further down the page, because the moment someone is deciding whether to fill in a form is exactly when they want reassurance, not before or after. If you work with recognisable clients, a named outcome, or hold a relevant security or compliance credential, that belongs within eye line of the CTA itself.

Step 4: decide gated versus ungated deliberately, per asset

Gate content that proves depth someone would genuinely trade an email address for: a detailed benchmark report, a worked framework, a template with real substance behind it. Leave explainer content and guides ungated, because their job is to earn organic traffic and build trust before someone is anywhere near ready to talk, and a form in front of them kills that job before it starts. Gating everything you publish quietly starves your organic funnel; ungating everything removes the one mechanism you have for capturing mid-funnel intent on the assets that deserve it.

Step 5: match CTA language to sales-cycle stage

A visitor on a top-of-funnel guide is not in the same frame of mind as one reading a pricing or comparison page. Exploratory language, "see how it works", "check if this fits", suits the first. Committed language, "book a consultation", "get a proposal", suits the second. Using the high-commitment phrasing everywhere makes early-stage pages feel like a hard sell before trust has been built, and using soft phrasing on a page a prospect reached specifically to buy just adds unnecessary friction at the exact moment they were ready to act.

According to First Page Sage's 2026 B2B benchmark analysis, the median B2B website conversion rate sits at 2.9%, with demo request pages specifically averaging closer to 1.8% because that ask carries genuinely more commitment than a newsletter signup or a guide download. Those figures are a useful check on whether a page has a real problem worth fixing, not a target to chase for its own sake. A page converting well below its category median after these five changes usually has a deeper issue with traffic quality or offer fit that no amount of CTA tuning will resolve on its own.

A worked example. One page, three stakeholders, one form.

Here is an illustrative scenario, built from the pattern I see repeatedly rather than any single client: a mid-market operations software company with a pricing page converting at roughly 1.1%, well under the 2.9% median for B2B pages. The page had one CTA, "request a demo", a five-field form asking for phone number and company size upfront, and no trust signals visible without scrolling past two full sections of feature copy.

Working through the five steps: the buying committee for this product is typically an operations lead and a finance approver, so the page needed something for both, not just a features list aimed at the operations lead alone. We kept "request a demo" as the primary CTA but added "see a two-minute walkthrough" as a lower-commitment second option, since a meaningful share of visitors were finance approvers not yet ready to sit through a live demo. We moved three client logos and one specific, named outcome up to sit directly beside the form, cutting the form itself to three fields: name, work email, and company. Pricing tiers stayed ungated, since hiding them would have driven evaluators straight to a competitor whose pricing was visible.

The judgement call worth naming here is the field count. Dropping phone number and company size from a first-touch form always raises the same objection internally, that it makes leads harder to qualify before a call. In practice, for a B2B audience with a genuine sales cycle ahead of it, that qualification happens naturally in the first conversation. The two extra fields were costing more completed forms than they were saving in wasted sales time, and that trade-off holds in most B2B contexts I have worked in, though it is always worth checking against your own funnel rather than assuming it applies uniformly.

Illustrative outcome aside, the mechanism is the one worth taking away: a graduated CTA, trust signals moved to where the decision actually happens, and a shorter form, applied together rather than as isolated tweaks, is what typically closes the gap between a page converting well below its category median and one converting at or above it. This is exactly the kind of diagnostic work covered in our B2B conversion rate optimisation engagements, where the starting point is always which of these five levers is actually broken, not a generic checklist applied without checking first.

Common mistakes. Where B2B pages lose visitors unnecessarily.

The most common mistake is treating the page like an ecommerce checkout: one CTA, one path, optimised purely for the visitor ready to convert right now. That approach ignores the reality that most B2B visitors, on a first or second visit, are not ready to book anything, and a page with no lower-commitment option simply loses them rather than converting them at a lower rate. If you want to understand how that first, "ready now" cohort differs from everyone else still evaluating, our piece on macro versus micro conversions covers the distinction in more depth.

The second is asking for information the sales team wants before the prospect has any reason to give it. Phone number, job title and budget range all belong later in the process, once a prospect is already talking to someone, not on the first form. Every field beyond what routing genuinely requires is a small, cumulative tax on your conversion rate.

The third is gating decisions made once, for every asset, rather than per piece of content. A blanket "everything goes behind a form" policy starves your organic search visibility, because ungated guides are what search engines and AI answer tools actually surface and cite. A blanket "nothing is gated" policy, meanwhile, throws away your only lever for capturing intent on the handful of assets substantial enough to earn an email address in exchange. Decide per asset, based on what that specific piece is trying to do.

The fourth is ignoring the fact that stakeholders read different evidence. A case study proving technical fit reassures an evaluator but does nothing for a budget holder who wants a number, a timeframe, or a comparison against the cost of doing nothing. Pages that only ever show one kind of proof are quietly failing to convince half the people who read them.

The fifth, and the one that undoes the other four if you skip it, is not measuring any of this properly before or after you change it. If you have not yet locked down your baseline, our guides to how to calculate conversion rate and what counts as a good conversion rate for your category are the starting point before you touch a single CTA. Change one lever at a time where you can, measure against that baseline over a full sales cycle rather than a week, and treat the mechanism, not any single number, as the thing you are actually optimising.

Common questions.

What is a good B2B website conversion rate for a demo request page?

First Page Sage's 2026 B2B benchmark report puts the median B2B website conversion rate at 2.9%, with demo request pages specifically averaging closer to 1.8% because that ask carries more commitment than a newsletter signup. A UK B2B services or SaaS page converting demo traffic above 3% is performing well against the market. Below 1.5%, look at form length, CTA commitment level and trust signals before assuming the traffic itself is the problem.

How many form fields should a B2B demo request form have?

Three to five fields is the workable range for a first-touch demo or consultation form: name, work email, company, and one qualifying question such as team size or the problem they are trying to solve. Fields asking for phone number, job title or budget at this stage measurably depress conversion because they read as sales qualification rather than a genuine next step. Ask for the rest once the prospect is already in a conversation with you.

Should B2B content be gated or ungated to improve conversion?

Neither answer is right for every asset. Gate content that proves depth a prospect will pay to access later, such as a detailed benchmark report or a worked framework, and ungate content whose job is to build trust and get found in search, such as guides and explainers. Gating everything suppresses your organic reach and top-of-funnel traffic; ungating everything removes your only mechanism for capturing mid-funnel intent before someone is ready to book a call.

How does a multi-stakeholder buying committee change website conversion tactics?

A buying committee means the person on your page today is rarely the only person who needs convincing, and often is not the final decision maker. Pages need a way for that visitor to take something credible back to colleagues, a one-page summary, a shareable case study, or a proposal they can forward, rather than assuming the single visitor in front of you controls the decision. Trust signals also need to speak to more than one role: a technical buyer and a budget holder are reassured by different evidence on the same page.

What CTA works best for early-stage B2B visitors who are not ready to book a demo?

A graduated, lower-commitment option next to the primary CTA works better than a single hard ask. A short assessment, a comparison guide, or a specific resource tied to the page topic gives an early-stage visitor a next step that matches their actual readiness, while the primary demo or consultation CTA stays visible for the smaller share of visitors who are ready now. Forcing every visitor down a single high-commitment path loses the majority who are still evaluating.
