Lean process improvement. Where the waste actually hides.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.16 November 20267 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a process and operations consultant for founder-led teams. More on her background at the about section.

Lean process improvement removes the steps in a process that cost time or money without adding anything the customer values, using eight waste categories (DOWNTIME) to find where it hides, then testing the fix in a small cycle before rolling it out. It began on a factory floor, but waiting and idle talent show up as often in an office.

The short answer. Removing what the customer never asked for.

Lean process improvement started with Taiichi Ohno's work at Toyota, where it was built around a simple distinction: a step in a process either adds value the customer would pay for, or it doesn't. Anything in the second category is waste, no matter how necessary it feels to the person doing it, and the discipline is finding it and removing it in small, tested steps rather than redesigning everything at once.

Ohno's original framework named seven wastes; practitioners later added an eighth as the method spread beyond manufacturing. Together they form the mnemonic DOWNTIME: defects, overproduction, waiting, non-utilised talent, transportation, inventory, motion and extra-processing. The letters are a memory aid, not a rulebook. What matters is using them as a checklist to look at a process you already run every week and notice what you've stopped seeing.

"Lean" and "process improvement" get used loosely enough that it's worth being precise: process mapping shows you what currently happens, and standard operating procedures lock in what should happen once it's agreed. Lean is the layer in between, the method for deciding which steps on that map genuinely need to stay.

It's also worth separating lean from the idea of simply "cutting costs". Cost-cutting usually removes headcount or spend first and asks questions about the process afterwards, if at all. Lean works the other way round: it looks at what the process actually does, decides which parts the customer is genuinely paying for, and only then asks what that implies for cost, time or headcount. The saving is a result of understanding the process properly, not a target set in advance that the process then gets forced to hit.

How it works in practice. Finding waste with a checklist, not a guess.

Run any process you own, a client onboarding sequence, an invoice approval chain, a monthly reporting cycle, against the eight categories below before assuming you already know where the problem is.

WasteWhat it looks like outside a factory
DefectsWork that has to be redone because it was wrong the first time: a contract sent with the wrong figures, a report rebuilt because a formula broke
OverproductionProducing more than the next step needs: a weekly report nobody reads weekly, a deck built with forty slides when five get presented
WaitingAn approval sitting in someone's inbox, a client waiting on a signature, a handover stalled because the next person is in back-to-back meetings
Non-utilised talentA skilled person doing data entry a junior hire or a simple automation could do just as well
TransportationA file, a customer record or a request moving between people or systems more times than the task actually requires
InventoryA backlog of unprocessed requests, half-finished onboarding records or unreviewed applications sitting idle
MotionSwitching between six systems to complete one task because nothing is connected
Extra-processingPolishing a document to a standard nobody downstream needs or will ever check

Defects and rework are the easiest waste to spot, because someone usually complains about them out loud. Waiting and non-utilised talent are harder to see, because they look like normal working life rather than a problem, and in most processes I've mapped they cost more in total than the defects everyone already knows about.

Once the eight categories have surfaced more candidates than you can fix at once, which they usually do, prioritise by multiplying frequency against cost rather than fixing whatever is most visible first. A defect that happens twice a year and takes an hour to correct matters less than a waiting point that adds two days to every single client file, even though the defect is the one someone is more likely to mention in a meeting. Rank the list before acting on it, and let the ranking, not the loudest complaint, decide what gets fixed first.

Once a waste is identified, resist the urge to fix everything at once. Lean's smaller discipline, the PDCA cycle (plan, do, check, act), tests one change against one metric for a short period, checks whether it actually worked, and only then rolls it out further. A process map built with process mapping gives you the "where" before you start; PDCA gives you the discipline for testing the fix without breaking something else in the process that was working fine.

What good looks like. A worked example outside a factory floor.

A professional services firm I worked with, illustrative rather than a named client, ran a new-client onboarding process that took an average of eleven working days from signed contract to the client's first working session. Nobody thought it was broken, because no single step looked wrong in isolation.

Mapping it against DOWNTIME told a different story. The actual working time inside the process was under four hours. The other ten and a half days were waiting: for a manager's sign-off on the account setup, for finance to issue the first invoice before anyone would schedule the kick-off, and for a welcome pack that a senior consultant was manually assembling by hand each time, despite it being near-identical for every client.

The fix wasn't a new system. It was removing three waiting points: the sign-off moved to an automatic approval under a set contract value, the invoice and the kick-off scheduling were decoupled so one no longer blocked the other, and the welcome pack became a template a junior team member could assemble in fifteen minutes instead of a senior person building it from scratch. Time from contract to first session dropped to four working days within one PDCA cycle, without adding a single new tool.

Two details from that project are worth keeping. First, the senior consultant who used to assemble the welcome pack by hand had never been asked whether the task actually needed her seniority, she had simply always done it, which is exactly how non-utilised talent accumulates in a business that's growing quickly and nobody has time to question. Second, the fix was tested on five new clients before it replaced the old process entirely, which caught a gap the first version missed: the automatic sign-off threshold had been set too low and was waving through a handful of deals that genuinely needed a manager's eyes. A smaller test cycle found that before it reached every client, not after.

Bain & Company's published case on an anonymised food manufacturer it calls FoodCo found something structurally similar at a very different scale: combining lean principles with Six Sigma's DMAIC method across forty-five ranked initiatives produced a 15% decrease in cost alongside a 25% increase in overall equipment effectiveness and a 30% increase in capacity. The company didn't start with forty-five fixes. It started by finding and ranking the waste, the same way the onboarding example above started with naming three waiting points rather than redesigning the whole process.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where lean attempts quietly stall.

The first pitfall is chasing the waste that is easiest to see. Defects and visible rework get fixed first because someone complains about them, while waiting and non-utilised talent, which usually cost more across a full process, get left alone because nobody frames them as a problem in the first place.

The second is treating lean as a one-off event rather than a standing discipline. A single improvement workshop that produces a new process map and then nothing for a year isn't lean, it's a one-time fix that will decay the same way the original process did. The businesses that keep the gains run short cycles routinely, not as an annual project.

The third is assuming the language only applies to a factory. "Lean" built its vocabulary on a production line, and that puts some service businesses off before they've checked whether the underlying idea, remove what the customer never asked for, applies to them. It almost always does.

The fourth is running the improvement without the people who actually do the work. Whoever processes the approvals or assembles the welcome pack usually knows exactly where the waiting and the rework sit, long before a map confirms it. Leaving them out of the exercise means missing the fastest route to the answer, and it tends to produce a fix nobody on the ground actually trusts enough to follow.

The fifth is skipping the small test cycle because the fix feels obvious. It rarely is, not completely. The onboarding fix above was the right direction and still shipped with a threshold set wrong the first time. Testing on five clients before five hundred is what turned an obvious-sounding idea into one that actually held up, and that discipline matters more as the change gets larger, not less.

None of this requires a certification or a consultant to start. The checklist above, run honestly against one process you already own, will usually turn up more than enough waste to justify a first PDCA cycle on its own. The discipline is the hard part, not the framework: naming the waste out loud, testing the fix small, and coming back to look again once the rest of the business has had time to forget the problem was ever there.

Common questions.

What is lean process improvement?

Lean process improvement is a method for removing the steps in a process that cost time or money without adding anything the customer actually values, then testing the fix in a small cycle before rolling it out. It comes from the Toyota Production System and is built around identifying and removing eight categories of waste.

What are the eight wastes in lean, also known as DOWNTIME?

DOWNTIME is a memorable shorthand for defects, overproduction, waiting, non-utilised talent, transportation, inventory, motion and extra-processing. The original seven wastes came from Taiichi Ohno's Toyota Production System; non-utilised talent was added later as the framework spread beyond manufacturing.

Is lean process improvement only for manufacturing?

No. The eight wastes translate directly to service and office work: waiting shows up as an approval sitting in someone's inbox, motion shows up as switching between six systems to complete one task, and extra-processing shows up as a report built to a higher standard than anyone downstream will use.

How is lean different from Six Sigma?

Lean focuses on removing waste and speeding up flow; Six Sigma focuses on reducing variation and defects using statistical analysis. Many practitioners combine them as Lean Six Sigma, using lean to simplify a process first and Six Sigma's DMAIC method to control the variation that remains.

How often should a business run a lean improvement cycle?

Treat it as a standing discipline rather than an annual event. Short PDCA cycles, a few weeks each, testing one change against one metric, hold up better than a single large improvement project run once a year and then left alone.

What is the biggest mistake businesses make with lean process improvement?

Chasing the waste that is easiest to see, usually defects or rework, while ignoring waiting and non-utilised talent, which are harder to spot but typically cost more over a full process. The second most common mistake is running one lean event and treating the process as fixed rather than returning to it.
