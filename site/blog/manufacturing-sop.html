Process mapping & SOP creation

A manufacturing SOP guide. Written for the floor, not the folder.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.26 October 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a process mapping and SOP consultant for founder-led and operations-heavy teams. More on her background at the about section.

A manufacturing SOP is a written, step-by-step instruction for one recurring production task, covering the steps, the quality check and the safety step, written so a trained operator can follow it without asking a supervisor. Good ones stay at the workstation and get used; most fail by sitting in a binder nobody opens.

The short answer. One task, one page, at the workstation.

A usable manufacturing SOP covers five things: the trigger that starts the task (a work order, a shift change, a changeover), numbered steps in the order they're actually performed, the quality or inspection checkpoint, the safety or PPE step, and a clear line for who to call when something falls outside the normal range. Leave out the escalation line and an operator is left guessing at exactly the moment a mistake becomes expensive.

PMMI's 2025 Engineering Best Practices report, drawn from 72 survey responses and six in-depth interviews with manufacturers, found that around 40% of companies rely on a database or system to capture tribal knowledge, yet interviews for the same report described that documentation as often inconsistent or out of date. That gap, having a system but not a current or trusted one, is the practical problem this guide is written to close. A manufacturing SOP only earns its keep if it describes the task as it's actually run today, not as it was run when the document was first written.

A usable manufacturing SOP generally covers:

  • The trigger. What starts the task, a work order, a shift handover, a scheduled changeover.
  • The steps. Numbered, in the order they're actually performed, not the order a flowchart would prefer.
  • The quality check. What to inspect, measure or confirm before moving on.
  • The safety step. PPE, lockout or a hazard-specific precaution, stated plainly, not buried in a general policy.
  • The escalation line. Exactly who to call when something falls outside the normal range.

How it works in practice. Write it at the machine, not from memory in an office.

The method is the same as any good SOP: watch or ask the person who actually runs the task to walk through it step by step, in the order they really do it, and note the exceptions they handle automatically, a jam, an out-of-spec part, a changeover that needs a tooling adjustment, before writing a single line. Our guide to creating an SOP covers that general method in more depth; this one focuses on what's different on a production floor.

Format should match the task, not a house template. A repetitive manual task, loading a machine, packing a carton pattern, usually works best as a single-page job aid with photos, kept physically at the workstation. A safety-critical or regulated step, lockout-tagout, an allergen changeover, a calibration check tied to a quality standard, needs a longer document with version control, a named owner and a sign-off, because an auditor or an incident investigation will eventually ask to see exactly which revision was in force on a given day.

Where regulatory or customer audits are part of the picture, build the review cycle into the document itself rather than relying on someone remembering to revisit it. A revision number, a last-reviewed date and a named owner printed on the page do more to keep an SOP current than any policy stating documents should be kept current.

What good looks like. An operator can follow it without finding a supervisor.

Test a draft SOP on someone trained on the equipment but not on this specific task. If they have to stop and find a supervisor more than once, the document is missing an exception or a visual cue, not a bigger explanation. Photos and simple diagrams do more work here than paragraphs; a line worker scanning a laminated sheet mid-task is reading for the next action, not studying prose. Given how often production roles turn over, an SOP that depends on reading comprehension under time pressure is one that quietly stops being followed within weeks.

It's also worth testing the document against a bad day, not just a normal one. A changeover SOP that only works when the right part arrives on time and the tooling is already calibrated isn't describing the job as it's actually run; it's describing the job on its best day, which is rarely the day anyone needs to consult the instructions at all.

Pitfalls to avoid. Documenting the happy path, losing the one person who knew the rest.

A manufacturing client I worked with, illustrative rather than a named result, had a changeover SOP that looked complete and had passed every audit for years. It covered the standard sequence cleanly. What it never captured was a small manual tension adjustment one long-serving technician made on an older line, something he'd done automatically for a decade and had never thought to mention because nobody had asked him to walk through the exception, only the standard procedure. When he left for another role, changeovers on that line started producing scrap again, and it took weeks of trial and error to rediscover what he'd known without writing down. The decision rule that follows: when you write an SOP, ask the person who runs it what they do when something's slightly off, not only what they do when everything's right, because that's usually the knowledge that walks out the door first.

A second, more common pitfall is version drift between shifts: day shift and night shift running from different printed revisions because nobody owns pulling the old copy down when a new one goes up. A dated, numbered revision pinned at the workstation, with the old copy physically removed, closes that gap for less effort than it sounds.

A third pitfall worth naming is writing the SOP once and never testing whether it survived contact with a real shift. A document that was accurate on the day it was signed off can drift out of date within months if a machine is serviced, a supplier changes a part's tolerance, or a line is reconfigured, and nobody is specifically responsible for checking the paperwork still matches the floor.

Getting started. Start with the task that hurts most when someone's away.

Rather than starting with the easiest task to write up, start with the one that causes the most disruption when the person who usually runs it is off sick, on leave, or has left. A short prioritisation exercise usually surfaces the right starting point faster than trying to document everything at once:

  • Which tasks currently depend on one named person's memory rather than a written step?
  • Which of those has the highest cost if done wrong, scrap, rework, a safety incident, a missed shipment?
  • Which is most likely to need running by someone new in the next twelve months?

That's usually where tribal knowledge is doing the most unseen work, and where documenting it first returns the most protection for the time spent. Our SOP creation work and process mapping service both start with exactly that kind of task-level audit before any document gets drafted.

Common questions.

What should a manufacturing SOP include?

Five things at minimum: the trigger that starts the task, numbered steps in the order they're actually done, the quality or inspection checkpoint, any safety or PPE step, and who to escalate to when something is out of spec. Skip the escalation step and operators improvise exactly when a mistake is most costly.

How detailed should a manufacturing SOP be?

Detailed enough that a trained operator who hasn't run this specific task before can complete it without finding a supervisor, and no more. A routine manual task usually needs a single page with photos; a safety-critical or regulated step, such as lockout-tagout or an allergen changeover, needs a longer document with a sign-off and a revision history.

Who should write manufacturing SOPs, the supervisor or the operator?

The person who actually runs the task should give the steps, including the exceptions they handle without thinking about it, while a supervisor or quality lead checks the draft against the standard and signs it off. An SOP written entirely from an office, without watching the task run, almost always misses the exception that matters most.

How often should manufacturing SOPs be reviewed?

On a fixed schedule, commonly annually, plus immediately after any equipment change, process change or near-miss involving that task. An SOP with no owner and no review date is the one most likely to still describe a machine or a step that no longer exists.
