CRM automation & workflow

Marketing tasks worth automating. Ten to start with, and the order that works.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.28 September 20266 min read

The marketing tasks worth automating first are the ones with a clear trigger and a repeatable action: lead scoring, welcome sequences, list segmentation, review requests, and internal handoffs to sales. Automating a task that still needs human judgement, personalised outreach to a top-tier prospect, for example, tends to backfire before it saves time.

Why this matters. The maths behind automating the right ten.

Marketing tasks to automate is the wrong question to start with if the answer is simply "as many as possible". HubSpot's 2026 State of Marketing report found that 47% of marketers already use automation to make marketing processes more efficient, and that the heaviest use sits in administrative work, 93% report automating scheduling, note-taking and documentation, rather than in judgement calls like which lead gets a personal outreach from a sales rep. That split matters: a founder-led team with a CRM that holds data but does not do the work is usually one honest audit away from finding five or six genuinely automatable tasks, not fifty. Our CRM automation work starts from exactly that audit, because automating the wrong task early tends to erode trust in the whole system faster than doing nothing would have.

A task qualifies for this list when it has both pieces: a trigger the CRM can detect on its own, a form fill, a date, a score crossing a number, and an action that does not vary by who receives it. A task with a clear trigger but a response that should vary by situation, an enterprise prospect getting a different next step than a self-serve signup, is a partial automation at best: the trigger can still fire, but the CRM should route to a person rather than sending a fixed message.

The list, with how to apply each. Ten tasks, and the trigger that starts each one.

  1. Lead scoring. Trigger: a new form fill or a behaviour signal such as repeat pricing-page visits. Action: automatically weight and route the lead by fit and engagement, rather than a rep manually reviewing every new contact.
  2. Welcome email sequences. Trigger: a new opt-in or signup. Action: a timed series that runs without anyone remembering to send the next email.
  3. List segmentation. Trigger: a data field changing, industry, deal stage, engagement level. Action: automatically move the contact into the matching list rather than relying on a manual export.
  4. Abandoned enquiry follow-up. Trigger: a form started but not finished, or a quote requested but never booked. Action: an automatic, low-pressure nudge after a set delay.
  5. Review requests. Trigger: a closed-won deal or a completed engagement. Action: a timed request for a public review, sent while the experience is still fresh.
  6. Event and webinar follow-up. Trigger: attendance or a no-show. Action: a tailored follow-up email specific to which of the two happened.
  7. Internal lead handoff to sales. Trigger: a lead score crossing an agreed threshold. Action: automatic notification to the right rep, with context attached rather than a bare name and email address.
  8. Re-engagement of cold contacts. Trigger: no recorded activity for a set period. Action: a scheduled win-back sequence rather than a marketer remembering to check the list.
  9. Reporting roll-ups. Trigger: end of week or month. Action: an auto-compiled dashboard rather than a manually rebuilt spreadsheet every reporting cycle.
  10. Social and content scheduling. Trigger: a content calendar entry. Action: automatic publishing at the set time, freeing the marketer from being online at the exact posting slot.

Sequence matters as much as the list itself. Start with the lowest-risk, most mechanical items, reporting roll-ups and content scheduling, since neither touches a customer directly and a wrong trigger just means a report looks odd for a week, not a wrong message landing in someone's inbox. Move next to lead scoring and list segmentation, which shape internal routing rather than anything a contact sees. Welcome sequences and the sales handoff come third, since these are customer-facing but run on well-understood triggers, a signup, a score threshold, that are easy to test before switching on. Save abandoned-enquiry follow-up and cold-contact re-engagement for last: both depend on a CRM field, time since last activity, form started but not finished, being genuinely accurate, and both are customer-facing enough that a wrong trigger reads as careless rather than merely inefficient. Teams that automate in the reverse order, customer-facing sequences first, before the data feeding them has been checked, are the ones most likely to end up in the mistake below.

Where teams go wrong. The automation mistakes that cost more than they save.

I worked with a SaaS founder whose team had automated a re-engagement sequence for "cold" contacts before fixing what counted as cold in their CRM. The field driving the automation, days since last activity, was not being updated by half their integrations, so the sequence fired against contacts who had, in fact, replied to an email the week before, through a channel the automation was not tracking. The fix was not more automation, it was tightening the trigger's data source first. That is the pattern I see most: teams reach for automation to solve a process problem that is actually a data problem, and the automation just makes the mistake happen faster and to more people.

  • Automating tasks that need a judgement call, rather than leaving those to a person who can read the specific situation.
  • Layering several automations that can fire on the same contact at once without checking whether their triggers overlap.
  • Automating before data hygiene is fixed, so a stale or wrongly updated field quietly drives the wrong action.
  • Setting and forgetting: building a sequence once and never reviewing how it actually performs months later.

Automate the six or seven tasks with a clean trigger and a repeatable action, and leave outreach that needs a human judgement call to a person. Common questions.

What marketing tasks should not be automated?

Anything that needs a judgement call: personalised outreach to a high-value prospect, handling a complaint, or a message responding to something specific a contact said. Automating these tends to read as impersonal at exactly the moment a human touch matters most, and usually costs more trust than the time it saves.

How many marketing tasks should a small team automate first?

Five to seven is a realistic starting point: lead scoring, a welcome sequence, list segmentation, review requests and the internal handoff to sales cover most of the clear-trigger, repeatable-action work. Adding more before these are running cleanly tends to create conflicting automations rather than extra time saved.

What percentage of marketers already use marketing automation?

47% of marketers report using automation to make marketing processes more efficient, according to HubSpot's 2026 State of Marketing report, with the heaviest use in administrative tasks such as scheduling and documentation rather than judgement-based work.

Can two marketing automations conflict with each other?

Yes, and it happens often. A welcome sequence and a sales handoff automation can both fire on the same contact within the same day if their triggers overlap, producing duplicate or contradictory messages. Reviewing trigger conditions across all active automations before adding a new one prevents most of this.

Should CRM data be cleaned before automating marketing tasks?

Yes. An automation is only as accurate as the field triggering it, and a stale or incorrectly updated data field will fire the automation against the wrong contacts. Fixing the underlying data hygiene first is usually a faster route to reliable automation than adding more rules on top of dirty data.
