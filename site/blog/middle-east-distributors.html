Hospitality tech & Middle East expansion

Finding distributors and partners in the Middle East. Vet the fit before you touch the contract.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.19 October 20269 min read

Finding distributors and partners in the Middle East works best through known channels: sector trade shows, chamber of commerce introductions and warm referrals from vendors already in-market, not cold outreach. Vet candidates hard before signing, because UAE's commercial agency law makes a registered agency far harder to exit than an ordinary distribution contract.

The short answer. Known channels beat a cold list, every time.

I'm Lauren Pearson, and most of the hospitality technology firms who ask me how to find a distributor in the Middle East have already tried the obvious route: a list of names from a search engine or a procurement directory, followed by cold emails that mostly go unanswered. That approach fails for a structural reason, not a persistence one. Distribution in this region still runs substantially on relationships and reputation within a small, visible set of trade shows and chambers of commerce; a name on a directory with no introduction behind it is competing against candidates who showed up to the same stand in person.

Our guide to partnerships for market entry covers the five models to choose between and how to structure the terms once you have a candidate in front of you. This piece is specifically about where that candidate actually comes from, and what to check before you get anywhere near a contract.

How it works in practice. Three channels that actually produce candidates.

Sector trade shows are still the highest-yield channel, provided you go to meet people rather than only to exhibit. GulfHost, Dubai World Trade Centre's hospitality equipment and foodservice technology event, ran its most recent edition from 26 to 30 January 2026 and is the closest match for sourcing hospitality tech distributors specifically. Arabian Travel Market, also at DWTC, held its most recent edition in September 2026 and drew more than 55,000 attendees from 166 countries, which makes it a useful hunting ground for travel and hospitality technology distribution more broadly. Walk the halls looking for stands already carrying complementary categories, point-of-sale, property management or guest experience tools that are not direct competitors, because those are the distributors who already have the sales relationships and after-sales capability your product needs.

Chambers of commerce and trade bodies, Dubai Chamber among them, run matching and introduction services aimed at exactly this problem, connecting an overseas vendor with vetted local distribution candidates rather than leaving you to cold-call a directory. These introductions move slower than a trade show conversation but come with a basic credibility check already done, which narrows the list before you spend time on due diligence.

Treat LinkedIn as a pre-trade-show research step, not a replacement for either channel above. Searching for people whose titles sit inside hospitality procurement, operations or technology at groups active in your target emirates will usually surface two or three names worth meeting in person at GulfHost or ATM rather than messaging cold; a warm face-to-face conversation booked ahead of the show converts at a far higher rate than a stand visit alone.

The highest-quality candidates, in my experience, come through a warm referral from a vendor already active in the market: a PMS or POS provider, a consultant, or another hospitality technology firm that is not a direct competitor, who can vouch for how a distributor actually performs day to day, not just how their pitch reads. Build this channel deliberately. Ask every other vendor conversation you have in the region who they would, or would not, work with again, and why.

Keep a short written log of every candidate conversation as you go, who introduced them, what they currently carry, and what the referring vendor said about them off the record, rather than trusting memory once you are three trade shows and a dozen names into the search. The log becomes the actual evidence base for the vetting pass below, and it is the first thing worth sharing with a local lawyer once you are down to a shortlist, so the legal review starts from facts rather than a fresh verbal summary of a conversation that happened months earlier. A dozen names sound similar after a long trip; the log is what stops two decent-but-different candidates blurring into one memory by the time you are back home deciding between them.

Expect this to run on a calendar measured in months, not weeks, built around the trade show cycle and the pace of chamber introductions. Firms that budget a single quarter for the whole process, sourcing through signature, usually end up rushing the vetting step to hit the date, which is the step that costs the most when it is skipped.

What good looks like. Vet the fit, then choose the legal structure deliberately.

Before any contract conversation, check five things properly: the candidate's existing portfolio and whether any line genuinely conflicts with yours, not just sits adjacent to it; their financial standing and how long they have actually been trading, not just registered; whether their geographic reach matches the specific emirates or countries you need, because "GCC coverage" on a pitch deck rarely means equal strength everywhere, a point our guide to the GCC hospitality market covers in more depth; their language and after-sales service capability in your actual target market; and two or three reference calls with their current principals, asked directly whether they would sign again.

Once a candidate clears vetting, the next decision matters as much as who you pick: whether you are registering the relationship as a commercial agency under UAE Federal Law No. 3 of 2022, effective 15 June 2023 and the successor to the older 1981 law, or signing an unregistered distribution contract instead. Registration is the gateway to the law's protections, and for a registered agency those protections run heavily in the agent's favour, exclusivity by default and a termination process that is genuinely difficult for the principal to exit without agreement or a proven, fundamental reason. An unregistered distribution contract, by contrast, is governed mainly by what you actually write into it, with exclusivity and termination terms you set yourselves rather than terms the law imposes; such arrangements generally sit under the UAE's Commercial Transactions Law instead. Neither option is automatically wrong, but signing one while believing you have agreed to the other is the mistake that costs firms years, not months, to unwind.

Two practical details catch firms out even after they have chosen the right structure deliberately. First, a registered agency contract has to be a written, notarised agreement, and a translated Arabic version is generally required alongside the English draft your head office actually negotiated; have both versions checked against each other by local counsel rather than assuming the translation is a formality. Second, registration is what unlocks the statutory protections in the first place, so an agency that was never actually registered with the Ministry of Economy does not get the exclusivity or termination protection the law describes, whatever the contract itself claims.

A worked scenario. One question, asked before signature, not after.

A hospitality technology vendor I advised was close to signing their first UAE distributor on the local lawyer's standard template, which registered the relationship as a full commercial agency under the 2022 law by default, because that was simply the firm's usual draft. Nobody on the vendor's side had asked whether that was the intended structure; they had assumed "distributor agreement" meant the lighter, more flexible arrangement they wanted for a first market test. Registering it as drafted would have made it genuinely difficult to exit if the distributor underperformed in year two, exactly the scenario the vendor was most worried about going in. We stopped before signature and asked the one question that mattered: are we registering this as an agency under the 2022 law, or signing an unregistered distribution contract with our own exclusivity and termination terms? The firm chose the unregistered route, with a written performance minimum and a defined renewal point, deliberately rather than by default.

This is an illustrative pattern from several market-entry engagements, not one named client, but the decision rule holds regardless of which lawyer drafts the contract: ask that question out loud before anyone signs, whatever the standard template says.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where Middle East distributor searches go wrong.

Signing the first candidate met at a trade show without the five-point vetting pass, because the conversation went well and the calendar is tight, is the most common mistake.

Registering a full commercial agency without understanding how hard it becomes to exit, or assuming an unregistered contract carries statutory protections it does not, is the second.

Treating "GCC distributor" as one relationship covering six countries equally is the third, when reach and strength usually varies sharply by market, as our guide to the GCC hospitality market sets out.

No written performance minimum is the fourth. A distributor who is simply not performing continues holding the territory because nothing in the contract defines what underperformance means or triggers a review.

And skipping reference checks because the introduction came through a trusted chamber or warm referral, treating the source's credibility as a substitute for checking the candidate directly, is the pitfall that catches even experienced teams. Common questions.

Where do I actually find hospitality technology distributors in the Middle East?

Through three channels mainly: sector trade shows such as GulfHost and Arabian Travel Market, chamber of commerce matching and introduction services, and warm referrals from other vendors already active in the market. A cold list pulled from a directory produces far fewer usable candidates than any of these three.

What's the difference between a distributor and a registered commercial agent under UAE law?

A registered commercial agent falls under UAE Federal Law No. 3 of 2022, which grants exclusivity by default and makes unilateral termination by the principal genuinely difficult. An unregistered distribution contract sits outside that statutory protection, with exclusivity and termination governed by whatever the two parties actually write into the agreement.

How long does finding and signing a Middle East distributor usually take?

Plan for months, not weeks. Sourcing through the trade show and chamber introduction cycle, vetting candidates properly and agreeing the legal structure typically runs a full quarter or more. Compressing that timeline is usually what causes the vetting step to get rushed.

Should I register my distributor relationship under UAE's commercial agency law?

Decide deliberately rather than by default. Registration suits a long-term, exclusive relationship you are confident in and willing to find hard to exit. A first market test with a new partner often fits better as an unregistered distribution contract with your own exclusivity and termination terms, reviewed with a local lawyer who confirms which structure your draft actually creates.

What should I check before signing with a Middle East distributor?

Five things: portfolio conflicts, financial standing and years actually trading, whether their geographic reach matches your specific target markets rather than "GCC" broadly, language and after-sales capability, and two or three direct reference calls with their current principals.

Can I work with more than one distributor across the GCC?

Often yes, and sometimes it is the better structure, since the GCC is not one market with equal distributor strength everywhere. A single exclusive distributor who is strong in the UAE but weak in Saudi Arabia can hold back growth in the market that matters most, which is worth checking before granting region-wide exclusivity to one partner.
