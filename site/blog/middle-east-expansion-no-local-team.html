Hospitality tech & Middle East expansion

Expanding into the Middle East without a local team. What you actually need in place, and what you don't.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.11 November 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a Dubai-based hospitality tech and GCC market entry consultant. More on her background at the about section.

You can expand into the Middle East without a local team by selling through a local agent or distributor rather than opening an office. Most hospitality technology firms entering the GCC this way still need one thing in place themselves: a named person who owns the relationship with that partner, because the partner is never a substitute for that.

The short answer. No office, but not no presence either.

Founders ask me this almost exactly the same way every time: "do we need a Dubai office to sell here, or can we do this remotely?" The honest answer is that you don't need an office, but you do need someone, somewhere, who owns the market, even if that person sits outside it on a plane every few weeks. The US Commercial Service's own country guide for the UAE confirms the practical route: a meaningful number of foreign companies sell into the market through a local agent or distributor rather than setting up a subsidiary, and plenty do it successfully without ever opening a physical office.

What's changed recently is how much flexibility a foreign company actually has if it does want to stay closer to the market without going all the way to a full office build-out. Several recent regulatory guides note that foreign investors can now hold full ownership of a mainland UAE company without a local Emirati sponsor across most sectors, a real relaxation from the older rule that required 51% local ownership for an onshore business. That doesn't make the no-office route obsolete, it just means a company isn't forced into a distributor relationship by ownership rules alone any more; the choice is now closer to a genuine strategic decision than a regulatory necessity.

There's also a lighter-weight middle option that founders often don't know exists until they ask a lawyer directly: an Employer of Record can legally hire a single local salesperson or support contact on your behalf, with no entity of your own required, while a virtual office covers a registered local address for licensing or contractual purposes. Neither gives you full trading rights the way a mainland branch does, but both can put a genuinely named, locally based person in front of a GCC buyer well before the cost and time of a full entity set-up would be justified by the pipeline you actually have.

How it works in practice. The two routes in, and what each one actually costs you.

RouteWhat you keepWhat you give up
DistributorNo inventory, logistics or local employment risk; distributor usually funds the local sales effortMargin (distributors buy and resell at their own markup); limited visibility into the end client relationship
Commercial agentDirect line of sight to the client, since the agent sells on your behalf rather than resellingYou still own pricing, contracting and usually support, so the workload reduction is smaller than it first appears
Mainland branch or free zone entityDirect control of pricing, brand, product registration and client dataThe setup, licensing and ongoing compliance cost and time the "no local team" approach was meant to avoid

A distributor typically takes ownership of your product and resells it, which means they carry inventory and payment risk and, in exchange, usually keep a larger margin than an agent would. For hospitality technology specifically, this model works best for a product that's genuinely self-service to install and support, since the distributor is unlikely to invest in deep technical training unless the commercial upside clearly justifies it for them too.

A commercial agent sells under contract on your behalf, without taking ownership of the goods, usually for a commission. This keeps you closer to the end client and the actual terms of each deal, but it also means you're still doing more of the direct work than "no local team" might suggest, contracting, pricing approval, often first-line support, just without your own staff physically in the country doing it.

The detail that catches hospitality tech vendors out most often is the free zone and mainland split. A free zone company gets real tax and ownership advantages, but generally can't trade directly with mainland clients without going through a mainland-registered distributor or setting up its own mainland branch. A vendor that assumes a free zone entity gives it full market access discovers the gap only once a mainland hotel group's procurement team asks for a locally licensed mainland counterparty the free zone entity can't provide on its own.

Timing and cost are the other variable founders consistently underestimate on both sides of this decision. Appointing a distributor or agent can be live within weeks once a suitable partner is found, with no licensing cost beyond the commercial terms agreed; a mainland branch or free zone entity typically takes several weeks to a few months to establish and carries ongoing licensing, visa and office costs regardless of how much revenue the market produces in year one. The right route isn't fixed, it depends on how confident the pipeline forecast already is: a company still validating GCC demand is usually better served starting with a partner and proving the market before committing to the fixed cost of an entity, while a company that already has confirmed enterprise demand may find the entity route pays for itself faster than the margin given away to a distributor would suggest.

What good looks like. A worked scenario on underestimating what "no office" still requires.

A hospitality technology vendor I worked with, illustrative rather than a named client, had decided to enter the GCC purely through remote sales calls run from its head office, reasoning that a cloud product needed no local presence at all to sell or support. The product itself was genuinely fine for that approach. What wasn't fine was procurement. Several GCC hotel groups the vendor was pursuing expected a locally contactable point of escalation as a baseline procurement requirement, not a nice-to-have, and a sales call from a time zone six or more hours away, with no named local contact for support issues, read as a company unwilling to commit to the region rather than one being efficient about it.

The fix wasn't opening an office. It was appointing a single local commercial agent with the authority to be the named point of contact for pricing conversations and first-line escalation, while the vendor kept product, engineering and second-line support entirely remote. The decision rule that came out of it: pick the one function that a GCC buyer expects to be locally reachable, almost always commercial and first-line support, rather than engineering, and put a real local name against that function before pursuing enterprise-level hospitality accounts. Everything else can stay remote for considerably longer than founders usually assume.

Within two procurement cycles of making that change, the vendor closed its first two mainland hotel-group contracts, both of which had stalled for months beforehand on exactly the same objection: no one locally reachable if something went wrong at 2am during a property's peak season. The product hadn't changed. The pricing hadn't changed. What changed was a GCC buyer no longer having to take the vendor's word for it that remote support would be responsive enough, because there was finally a local name and number attached to the answer.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where the "no local team" approach quietly breaks.

The first pitfall is confusing a distributor relationship with a go-to-market strategy. Appointing a distributor is a channel decision, not a substitute for having someone on your own side who understands the region, prioritises it in quarterly planning, and notices when the distributor's effort is quietly drifting elsewhere.

The second is letting the distributor or agent hold the product registration, local licensing or the primary client relationship with no written reversion plan. If the relationship ends, and regional partnerships end more often than founders expect, untangling a registration or a client list that sits entirely with the former partner can set a market entry back by a year or more.

The third is assuming a free zone entity gives full market access. It gives real benefits, but mainland trading restrictions are a genuine constraint, not a technicality, and a procurement team that specifically needs a mainland-licensed counterparty will not accept a free zone entity as a workaround, however the product itself performs.

The fourth is treating every GCC market as interchangeable with the UAE. A distributor relationship, ownership rule or licensing requirement that applies in the UAE does not automatically transfer to Saudi Arabia, Qatar or the wider GCC, each of which runs its own rules on foreign ownership, sponsorship and market access, and a plan built entirely around UAE research will need real rework, not a light edit, before it holds up anywhere else in the region.

The fifth, the one I see most often with hospitality tech specifically, is under-resourcing the relationship with the local partner itself. A distributor given a product, a price list and no further contact for six months behaves exactly as under-prioritised as it's been treated, and the fastest way to accelerate a remote-first entry is usually a tighter, more frequent cadence with the one local partner already appointed, not another layer of infrastructure stacked on top of it.

The sixth is choosing the entry route around personal comfort with the region rather than the product's actual local support needs. A founder who enjoys travelling to Dubai regularly can mistake frequent personal visits for a functioning local presence, when what a GCC buyer actually needs is a consistently reachable point of contact between those visits, not a charismatic founder who shows up well but disappears for ten weeks at a time. The right structure survives the founder being unavailable; a structure that depends on one person's travel calendar isn't a local presence, it's a scheduling risk wearing a local presence's clothes.

Common questions.

Can I sell into the UAE without opening a local office?

Yes. The US Commercial Service's own UAE country guide notes that many foreign firms sell into the market by appointing a local agent or distributor rather than opening an office or subsidiary. The trade-off is that you give up some direct control over pricing, positioning and the client relationship in exchange for faster, lower-cost access.

What is the difference between a distributor and an agent in the UAE?

A distributor typically buys your product outright and resells it, carrying inventory risk and usually earning a larger margin as a result. A commercial agent sells on your behalf under contract, without taking ownership of the goods, usually for a commission, and tends to leave you with more visibility into the end client relationship.

Do I still need a local sponsor to operate in the UAE?

For most onshore business activities, no. Foreign investors can now hold full ownership of a mainland UAE company without a local Emirati sponsor across the majority of sectors, a significant relaxation of the older rule that required 51% local ownership. Some strategically sensitive activities still carry sponsor or approval requirements, so it is worth confirming your specific activity against the current list rather than assuming the general rule applies.

Can a free zone company sell directly to mainland UAE clients?

Generally not without going through a local distributor or setting up a mainland branch. Free zone status gives a business tax and ownership advantages but restricts direct mainland trading, which is one of the main reasons hospitality tech vendors end up working through a mainland-based partner even once they have a free zone entity.

What's the biggest risk of entering through an agent or distributor?

Losing visibility and control over the client relationship and, in some markets, the product registration itself. If the partner holds the registration or the primary client contact, replacing them later becomes far harder than it would have been to negotiate those terms up front.
