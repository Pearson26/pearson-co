Customer journey mapping

Customer journey personalisation. Done by stage, not by name token.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.27 November 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a growth and CRM consultant for founder-led and SaaS teams. More on her background at the about section.

Customer journey personalisation means changing what a prospect sees, reads or hears based on their actual stage in the journey and their buyer type, not inserting their first name into a generic message. It needs a clean lifecycle stage field and two or three written buyer segments far more than it needs a bigger software platform.

The short answer. Personalise by stage and segment, not by name token.

I'm Lauren Pearson, and I still see "personalisation" used to describe two very different things. One is a mail-merge field that drops a first name into a subject line. The other is changing what someone actually sees based on where they genuinely are in their decision and what kind of buyer they are, which is the version that moves a number. A first-name token is not personalisation. It is formatting.

Real personalisation starts with the journey map, not the CRM field list. Once you know the stages a buyer moves through, awareness, consideration, decision, onboarding, retention, and you know the two or three buyer archetypes who move through them differently, you have the two inputs that actually change what gets sent. Everything else, the platform, the dynamic content blocks, the AI-written variants, is execution detail that only matters once those two inputs exist.

McKinsey's 2026 Global B2B Pulse survey, drawing on nearly 4,000 decision-makers across 13 countries, found that B2B buyers now move across an average of ten touchpoints during a purchase and consistently cluster into a small number of recurring buyer archetypes across industries. That second finding matters more than the first for personalisation specifically: a handful of real buyer types, not a long tail of individual preferences, is what most personalisation programmes should actually be built around.

Ten touchpoints also means a single personalised email rarely does the whole job on its own. The stage and the segment need to hold steady across every one of those touchpoints, the ad, the landing page, the sales call, the proposal, rather than being personalised once at the top of the funnel and left generic everywhere after. A buyer who gets a sharp, problem-specific first email and then a completely generic follow-up call notices the inconsistency, and it reads as a marketing trick rather than a business that actually understands their situation.

How it works in practice. Stage first, segment second, channel last.

Start with the stage. Awareness-stage content should personalise by the problem and the industry the visitor appears to have, not by product, because naming your product to someone who has not yet agreed they have the problem reads as a pitch rather than help. A hospitality operator searching for "guests not returning after their first stay" wants to see their own problem described back to them, not a feature list.

Consideration-stage personalisation can safely narrow toward product fit: which use case this visitor's behaviour suggests, which competitor they likely compared you against based on referral source, which objection they are probably sitting on given their company size or sector. This is where a journey map built with named stages and known drop-off points earns its keep, because it tells you exactly which page or email is doing the consideration-stage work and what it currently assumes about the visitor reading it.

Decision and onboarding stages can personalise by buying-committee role, pricing tier and integration complexity, the detail a buyer actually needs once they are evaluating seriously rather than browsing. Retention-stage personalisation shifts again, toward usage data: what a specific account has actually done in the product, not what segment they were assigned to at sign-up, since behaviour six months in tells you more than a static label ever will.

None of this needs a large platform to begin. A CRM with a reliable lifecycle stage field, synced with your customer journey map, and two or three written buyer segments will get a founder-led team further than an expensive personalisation engine layered on top of messy, unsegmented data. The segmentation and the stage definitions are the hard part. The send mechanism is not.

Channel choice follows the same stage logic rather than running on its own schedule. An awareness-stage visitor who has read one article does not need a retargeting sequence built for someone three calls into a procurement process, and a decision-stage buyer close to signing does not need another generic newsletter alongside the specific pricing and integration detail they actually asked their champion for. Mapping which channel each stage genuinely uses, rather than firing every channel at every contact because the platform allows it, is what keeps a personalisation programme from turning into noise with a customer's name stitched into it.

Measurement has to match the stage too. An awareness-stage email earns its keep on open and reply rates to a problem-framed message, not on immediate pipeline. A decision-stage personalisation effort should be judged on whether the right stakeholder saw the right detail before a renewal or close date, which is a harder thing to track than a click-through rate but the only measure that actually tells you the personalisation did its job. Teams that judge every stage by the same bottom-of-funnel metric end up optimising awareness content for a number it was never meant to move, and then conclude personalisation does not work when the real problem was the scorecard.

What good looks like. A small set of real variants, used consistently.

Good personalisation looks boring from the inside. A handful of named segments, each with a written one-line description, each mapped to a specific set of messages per stage, reviewed and updated on a set schedule rather than left to drift. Nobody is writing a fresh message for every lead; they are choosing correctly from a small, deliberate set and trusting the set rather than constantly expanding it.

It also looks consistent across teams. A sales rep picking up a lead that marketing nurtured through the consideration stage should see the same segment label and the same stage the lead's content history implies, not a different system's guess at who this person is. When the CRM, the journey map and the marketing platform agree on stage and segment, a handover between teams does not reset the personalisation to zero.

I worked with an illustrative SaaS team, not a named client, whose outbound emails were identical regardless of whether the recipient had never heard of the category or had already read three comparison articles. Once we split the list by the two signals already sitting in their CRM, content engaged with (a proxy for stage) and company size (a proxy for buyer archetype), the awareness-stage segment got a problem-framed email with no product mention, and the consideration-stage segment got a direct comparison against the two competitors their referral traffic suggested they were already evaluating. Replies from the consideration segment picked up noticeably, and more usefully, the sales team stopped fielding "what do you actually do" questions from leads who had supposedly already been nurtured for weeks.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where personalisation efforts quietly stall.

The first and most common mistake is personalising the channel before the message, running the same generic content through email, retargeting ads and a chatbot and calling the multi-channel delivery "personalised" because it reaches someone in several places. Reach is not relevance. The message itself has to change by stage and segment, or the channel variety is just noise delivered more often.

The second is building too many segments to maintain. A founder-led team that defines nine buyer personas usually cannot remember which version of the pricing page goes to which one within a quarter, and the personalisation quietly reverts to whichever version someone last edited. Two or three segments, written down and actually used, beat nine segments nobody can recall.

The third is treating the sign-up segment as permanent. A buyer assigned to "enterprise" at the top of the funnel based on company size might behave nothing like a typical enterprise account once they are inside the product, and retention-stage personalisation that still relies on that original label is personalising against stale information. Oxford Economics' 2023 research for Adobe, surveying 1,500 executives and 4,000 customers, found weak predictive analytics capability and incomplete customer data were the recurring reasons personalisation efforts stalled well short of full coverage, a pattern that has not meaningfully changed since: the data discipline is the bottleneck, not the ambition.

The fourth is mistaking a dynamic content block for a personalisation strategy. Swapping an image or a headline based on a cookie is a tactic, not a stage-and-segment framework, and it tends to collapse the moment someone looks closely at whether the swapped content actually matches where that visitor really is in their decision.

The fifth is building the segments once and never revisiting them as the business changes. A segment written when a company sold one product to one type of buyer stops describing reality once a second product line or a new market brings in a genuinely different buyer, and personalisation built on the old segment definitions starts quietly misfiring, not because the mechanism broke but because the inputs it relies on went stale. Revisiting the segment definitions on the same schedule you revisit the journey map itself, at minimum twice a year, catches this before a prospect notices the message no longer fits them.

Our customer journey mapping work starts with naming the real stages and the real segments before touching a single email template or landing page, because the template is the easy part once those two things are settled honestly.

Common questions.

What is customer journey personalisation?

It is tailoring what a prospect or customer sees, a page, an email, a sales conversation, to their actual stage in the journey and their buyer type, rather than sending the same generic message to everyone or simply inserting their first name into a template. The stage matters more than any other single input.

Do I need a big marketing automation platform to personalise the journey?

No. A CRM with a clean lifecycle stage field and a short, written definition of two or three buyer segments will get a founder-led team further than an expensive platform layered on top of messy, unsegmented data. The data discipline matters more than the tool.

What is the biggest barrier to personalising a customer journey?

Data, not technology. Oxford Economics' 2023 research for Adobe, covering 1,500 executives and 4,000 customers globally, found only around 40% of organisations had achieved personalisation across their customer base, with incomplete customer data and weak predictive capability named as the recurring blockers rather than a lack of software.

Should every stage of the journey be personalised the same way?

No. Awareness-stage content should personalise by problem and industry, not by product, since mentioning your product too early reads as a pitch rather than help. Later stages can personalise by product fit, pricing tier and buying committee role, where product-specific detail is exactly what the buyer is looking for.

How many buyer segments should a founder-led team start with?

Two or three, written down in a sentence each, covering the segments that behave differently enough to need a different message or offer. A longer list sounds thorough but usually collapses in practice, because nobody can remember which version of the message goes to which segment, so the personalisation quietly reverts to one generic version.

Is personalisation the same as segmentation?

Segmentation groups people by shared traits; personalisation is what you do with that grouping, changing the message, offer or next step for each group. A business can segment its customers perfectly and still fail to personalise anything if the segmentation never changes what anyone actually sees or receives.
