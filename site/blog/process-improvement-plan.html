Process mapping & SOP creation

A process improvement plan. One page, filled in properly.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.27 November 20267 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a growth and operations consultant for founder-led teams. More on her background at the about section.

A process improvement plan is a single-page document: a written problem statement, the current process as it actually runs, a root cause, a specific fix with a named owner and date, a measurable target, and a date to check the result. It works because every field forces a decision someone can later be held to.

What the template covers. Six fields, not a project charter.

I'm Lauren Pearson, and most "process improvement plans" I see are either a single bullet point someone wrote in a meeting and never looked at again, or a thirty-slide project charter nobody but the project manager reads. Neither one gets the process fixed. The version that actually works sits in between: short enough to fill in during one working session, specific enough that someone can be held to it three months later.

FieldWhat goes in it
Problem statementOne sentence, the symptom, who it affects, and roughly how often
Current processThe steps as they actually run today, not as the SOP says they should
Root causeThe one or two reasons the problem actually happens, not a list of everything wrong
The changeA specific action, not a direction of travel
Owner and dateOne named person, one date the change goes live
Success target and check dateA number or a clear yes/no, checked on a fixed date

The problem statement is the field most plans get wrong by skipping the "how often" detail. "Invoicing is slow" tells nobody anything useful. "Invoices go out an average of nine days after a job completes, against a five-day target, on roughly a third of jobs" gives you something to actually measure against later, and it usually reveals the real size of a problem that felt bigger or smaller than it is when it was only a complaint in a meeting.

The root cause field is where most plans either get too vague or too long. A list of twelve contributing factors is not a root cause, it is an excuse not to decide which one matters most. Pick the one or two causes that, if fixed, would remove most of the problem, and write the rest off as acceptable background noise for this round. You can always run a second plan later for whatever is left.

How to use it. One process, one owner, one working session.

Pick one process, not the five that are all bothering someone this quarter. Processes compete for the same limited attention and follow-up time, and a plan split across five things rarely gets the checking step that actually closes the loop on any of them. Choose the process costing the most time or the most client frustration right now, and leave the rest for a second plan once this one is done.

Map the current process as it actually runs before writing anything else. Our own process mapping work exists because the gap between the documented version and the real version is usually where the actual problem hides, a sign-off step everyone skips under deadline, a handoff that only works because one specific person remembers to chase it. Writing the plan against the documented version instead of the real one fixes a process that does not exist.

Name one owner, not a committee, and give them an actual date, not "this quarter". A plan with a committee as its owner has no one person whose job it is to notice the check date arrived, and "this quarter" has a way of quietly becoming next quarter once something more urgent shows up. Thirty to ninety days is a realistic window for most operational processes: long enough to see the change run several times under real conditions, short enough that the people involved still remember what the original problem actually was when you sit down to check it.

Set the success target before the change goes live, not after you see how it performed. A target set after the fact tends to quietly match whatever number came out, which defeats the purpose of having a target at all. Write it down, put a date against it, and put that date in a calendar, not just at the bottom of the document where it is easy to forget.

A worked example. A 90-day plan for a late-invoicing process.

An illustrative professional services firm, not a named client, had a quiet but persistent complaint: finance kept chasing project leads for sign-off before an invoice could go out, and clients were noticing the delay. The plan's problem statement read: invoices go out an average of nine days after job completion against a five-day target, on about a third of jobs, concentrated among three senior project leads who travel often.

Mapping the current process showed the real cause was not finance being slow, which is what most people in the business assumed. It was a sign-off step that required the project lead's explicit approval by email, with no fallback if they were travelling or simply behind on their inbox. The root cause written into the plan was narrow and specific: no fallback approver when the primary lead is unavailable within 48 hours.

The change was equally specific: any invoice sign-off outstanding after 48 hours automatically routes to a named deputy with authority to approve, and the project lead is notified rather than asked. The owner was the operations manager, the live date was set for the following Monday, and the success target was invoices going out within five days on at least 90% of jobs, checked exactly 60 days later. At the check date, the business was hitting the five-day target on most jobs, a meaningful improvement on the original third, and the three travelling leads who had caused most of the original delay were no longer the bottleneck, because the deputy routing caught exactly the cases they used to hold up.

What made the plan work was not the fix itself, which was fairly obvious once the real cause was named. It was that the plan forced someone to write down the 48-hour threshold, the named deputy, and the 60-day check date before the change went live, so there was something concrete to measure against rather than a vague sense that invoicing had got a bit better.

Common questions.

What should a process improvement plan include?

A written problem statement, the current process mapped as it actually runs, a root cause rather than a symptom, a specific action with a named owner and a date, a measurable success target, and a fixed date to check whether it worked. Leave any field out and the plan tends to drift into a general intention rather than a tracked change.

How is a process improvement plan different from a process map?

A process map shows the steps a process currently follows. A process improvement plan documents a specific change to that process, why it is needed, who owns it, and how you will know by a set date whether it worked. Most processes benefit from a map first and a plan second.

How many processes should a small team improve at once?

One, or at most two, run to completion before starting a third. Teams that open plans on five processes simultaneously rarely finish any of them properly, because attention and follow-up time split across all five and none gets the checking step that actually closes the loop.

What is a realistic timeframe for a process improvement plan?

Thirty to ninety days for most operational processes in a founder-led business, long enough to see the change run several times under real conditions, short enough that the original problem and the people involved are still fresh when you check the result.

Who should own a process improvement plan?

One named person, not a committee. A plan with a committee as its owner usually has no one actually accountable for whether the check date happens, while a single named owner has somewhere to put the follow-up question when the date arrives.
