Process mapping & SOP creation

Business process mapping tools. Pick the tool after the method, not before.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.16 September 20268 min read

The main business process mapping tools are Lucidchart, Microsoft Visio, Miro and draw.io (diagrams.net). Lucidchart suits a team building a central library of finished maps, Visio suits Microsoft-heavy organisations needing deep BPMN support, Miro suits live workshop mapping, and draw.io suits a founder-led team that just needs one process documented for free.

The short answer. Four tools, four different jobs.

Ask five founders which process mapping tool to use and you will get five different answers, usually whichever one a previous employer happened to use. That is the wrong way to choose. The tools genuinely differ in what they are built for, and matching the tool to the job saves more time than any feature comparison table on its own.

This is a narrower question than how to create a process map, which covers the method: choosing boundaries, interviewing the person who does the work, marking decisions and handoffs. That guide is deliberately software-agnostic, because the method matters more than the tool and rushing to a diagramming app before the sequence is understood is the single most common mistake in process mapping. This piece assumes the method is sorted and answers the next question: which tool actually holds the result.

It is also a different question from the types of process map, flowchart, swimlane, SIPOC, value stream map, BPMN, which is about notation and format, not software. Every tool covered here can draw every one of those formats; the difference between them is what happens around the drawing, not the shapes themselves.

How they differ. Built for the finished map, or built for the conversation.

The clearest way to split the four tools is by what they optimise for.

ToolBuilt forBest fitCost
LucidchartA central, maintained library of finished mapsA team documenting several processes that need version history and named ownersFree tier for basics; paid per-user plans beyond that
Microsoft VisioDeep, standards-based diagramming, including full BPMNOrganisations already on Microsoft 365 with technical or integration-heavy processesAdd-on licence within Microsoft 365, or standalone
MiroA live, shared canvas for mapping a process togetherThe workshop itself: interviewing a team and drafting the sequence in the roomFree tier for a single board; paid per-user plans for teams
Draw.io (diagrams.net)Free, no-frills diagramming with a standard shape libraryA founder-led team documenting one or two processes without budget for a subscriptionFree, open-source, no account required for basic use

Lucidchart's real strength for a growing business is less the drawing canvas itself and more what sits around it: a central repository where every process map a business has built lives in one place, with an owner assigned and a version history, rather than scattered across individual desktops as separate files nobody remembers to update.

Visio remains the enterprise default for a reason. Its BPMN support is the most complete of the four, built for processes that will eventually be read directly by workflow or integration software rather than only by people. That depth is genuinely wasted on a process a person will keep running by hand, which is most processes in a founder-led business.

Miro sits apart from the other three because it was not built primarily as a diagramming tool. It reached 100 million users across roughly 250,000 organisations by October 2025, and most of that growth came from teams using it as a shared workshop space, brainstorming, retrospectives, planning, with process mapping as one use case among many. Its BPMN templates and shape libraries are solid, but the tool feels, correctly, more like a whiteboard than a finished-document generator.

Draw.io is the tool most founder-led businesses actually need and most overlook, because it has no marketing budget behind it. It covers standard flowchart and swimlane notation cleanly, embeds into Google Drive and Confluence, and costs nothing. The trade-off is exactly what you would expect from a free tool: no built-in workshop features, no maintained central library, and a plainer interface than the paid alternatives.

Which to use and when. Match the tool to the stage the process is at.

If the process has never been mapped before and the goal is to get the actual sequence right, start with whichever tool makes it easiest to sit with the person who does the work and sketch as they talk, a physical whiteboard, Miro, or even paper. The drawing tool matters least at this stage; capturing what genuinely happens matters most.

Once the sequence is confirmed against a real case, the map needs a permanent home. For a single process or two, draw.io does the job at no cost. For a business building a genuine library, five, ten, twenty mapped processes that need to stay current and have a named owner each, Lucidchart's structure earns its subscription.

Visio only enters the picture once a process is heading towards automation, a workflow tool, or a system integration that needs to read the map's logic directly rather than a person reading it. Buying Visio before that point is buying capability nobody in the business will use.

How Lauren would decide. Start free, upgrade only when the free tool is the actual bottleneck.

With founder-led clients, I default to draw.io for the first process map every time, no exceptions, because a subscription decision should never be what stalls someone from documenting a process this week. If the business only ever needs one or two processes mapped, that is often where it stays permanently, and that is a perfectly good outcome.

The signal to upgrade to Lucidchart is not a feeling that the free tool looks basic, it is a specific, practical problem: two people editing the same file and overwriting each other's changes, or a map nobody can find because it is buried in someone's downloads folder from eight months ago. Once that happens more than once, the subscription pays for itself in the time it saves finding and reconciling files.

I only recommend Visio when a client is already committed to Microsoft 365 across the business and has a specific process heading toward automation, not as a general-purpose upgrade. A client of mine once bought a full Visio licence to draw three simple onboarding flowcharts a person would run manually forever; the licence sat mostly unused, and the same three flowcharts would have taken the same afternoon in draw.io for nothing. Buy the tool for the problem you actually have, not the one a heavier tool implies you might have later.

Common questions.

What is the easiest process mapping tool for a small business to start with?

Draw.io, now branded diagrams.net, for most founder-led teams. It is free, has no account wall for basic use, and its shape library covers standard flowchart and swimlane notation without a learning curve. Move to a paid tool only once the free option is genuinely limiting the work.

Is Microsoft Visio worth buying for process mapping?

Usually only if the business already runs on Microsoft 365 and needs BPMN notation for processes that will eventually connect to workflow or automation software. For a process a person will keep running by hand, Visio's extra depth rarely earns its added cost and licensing complexity.

What is the difference between Lucidchart and Miro for process mapping?

Lucidchart is built for the finished diagram: clean process maps, a central library, version history and defined ownership. Miro is built for the conversation that produces the diagram: a live workshop where a team maps a process together on a shared board. Many teams end up using Miro to draft and Lucidchart, or an equivalent, to formalise.

Do I need a paid process mapping tool at all?

Not to get started. A whiteboard, a free tier or even structured sticky notes will map a first process perfectly well. A paid tool earns its cost once several processes need to live in one place, be reviewed by more than one person, or connect into other software.

Which process mapping tool supports BPMN properly?

Visio has the deepest native BPMN support of the mainstream tools. Lucidchart and Miro both offer BPMN shape libraries and templates that cover most founder-led use cases, but neither matches Visio's depth for a process that needs to be read directly by specialised workflow or integration software.
