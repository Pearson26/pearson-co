Process mapping & SOP creation

Process standardisation. Making work repeat the same way, on purpose.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.20 November 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a process and revenue operations consultant for founder-led and multi-location teams. More on her background at the about section.

Process standardisation means the same task produces the same result regardless of who does it or where, through one agreed method that's written down, trained on and checked over time. It's different from writing a single SOP: standardisation is the ongoing work of getting a documented method actually adopted, measured and kept current as the business changes.

The short answer. One method, written down, actually followed.

Process standardisation gets confused with documentation more often than almost any other operations term. A business writes a handful of SOPs, feels the job is done, and is genuinely surprised a year later to find three different versions of the "standard" process running across three teams or three locations. Writing the document was never the hard part. Getting it adopted, checked and kept current as the business changes is, and that's the part standardisation actually refers to.

The clearest definition I use with clients: a process is standardised when you can ask two people doing the same job, separately, how they do it, and get the same answer on every step that actually affects the outcome. Not identical phrasing, not the same personality in how they explain it, but the same sequence, the same checks, the same result. If the answers diverge on something that changes quality, speed, compliance or what the customer experiences, the process isn't standardised yet, whatever document says it should be.

The discipline has a long, well-documented history outside of software and CRM tooling. Standardised work is one of the three pillars of the Toyota Production System, formalised by Taiichi Ohno and his colleagues through the 1960s to 1980s, and it rests on a principle that still holds for a ten-person services business: you cannot improve a process that isn't first performed the same way each time, because you can't tell whether a change made things better or whether you're just comparing two different methods against each other.

That last point is the one founders tend to miss when they dismiss standardisation as bureaucracy for bigger companies. A business with no fixed method for, say, onboarding a new client can't actually tell whether a change to the onboarding email sequence helped, because the last ten onboardings were each run slightly differently by whoever happened to handle them. Standardise the method first, then change one thing at a time, and the business can finally tell what's actually working rather than guessing from a mix of outcomes produced by several different approaches at once.

How it works in practice. Fix the steps that matter, not every step.

Good standardisation starts by separating steps that must never vary from steps that should stay flexible. Anything touching safety, legal compliance, data handling or a specific promise made to a client belongs in the first group and gets a fixed, non-negotiable method. Anything that depends on genuine situational judgement, how a salesperson phrases an objection response, which order a chef plates a dish that has no food-safety implication either way, belongs in the second group and should stay a guideline rather than a rule.

The ISO 9001:2015 quality management standard, one of the most widely adopted frameworks of its kind globally, is built on exactly this logic: it requires organisations to document and control processes that affect product or service quality, while explicitly leaving room for organisations to define their own methods rather than prescribing a single universal process. The standard doesn't ask a business to standardise everything. It asks a business to know which processes matter enough to control, and to actually control those ones properly.

A practical way to find that boundary is the APQC Process Classification Framework, maintained by APQC, a US-based benchmarking non-profit, since 1992, which groups business activity into a consistent set of process categories used across industries for benchmarking. You don't need to adopt the full framework to borrow its habit: group your own processes into families first, operating, customer-facing, support, and standardise within each family in order of how much a mistake in that family actually costs the business, rather than standardising whatever happens to be easiest to document first.

What good looks like. Multiple locations, one method, fewer surprises.

A hospitality group I worked with, illustrative rather than a named result, ran the same guest check-in process at several properties, each one originally set up by a different general manager with their own preferences. Two properties collected payment details before confirming the room was ready; one collected them after. On paper this looked trivial. In practice it meant the group's finance team saw payment timing data that genuinely wasn't comparable across properties, which made cash flow forecasting at group level far less reliable than anyone realised until someone finally asked why the numbers never quite reconciled.

Standardising that one step, fixing payment collection to happen at a specific point in check-in across every property while leaving the rest of the welcome experience to each property's own style, solved the finance problem without flattening the parts of the guest experience that were genuinely meant to differ by location. That's the pattern worth copying: find the one or two steps actually causing the downstream problem, standardise those specifically, and resist standardising the whole process just because you're already in the document.

Good standardisation also shows up in how exceptions get handled. A standardised process with no documented exception path just pushes people to quietly break the standard the first time a real edge case shows up, and once that happens once without consequence, the standard stops being trusted at all. A short, explicit list of known exceptions, and who's allowed to approve a new one, keeps the core process intact while still letting the business handle the cases it can't fully predict in advance.

It's also worth building in a plain way for the standard to improve from the bottom up. The people performing a standardised process daily usually spot a better method within weeks, long before any formal review cycle would surface it. A short-form channel, a single shared note, a five-minute slot in a weekly meeting, where someone can flag "I think step four should change, here's why", keeps the standard a living document rather than a fixed rule imposed once and never revisited until it visibly breaks.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where standardisation efforts stall.

The first pitfall is standardising from head office without input from whoever actually does the work. A process written by someone who hasn't performed the task recently tends to miss the real-world exceptions, and the people doing the job either quietly ignore the standard or burn time escalating edge cases the document never anticipated.

The second is treating the first version as final. A standard that hasn't been revised in two years isn't stable, it's stale, and the gap between what's written and what people actually do widens every month nobody checks.

The third is standardising for the current team size rather than the one you're growing into, or the reverse: building elaborate, multi-approval standards for a five-person team that don't earn their overhead until the business is five times the size. Size the rigour of the standard to the actual cost of getting that particular process wrong, not to how official you want it to feel.

The fourth is skipping the check step entirely. A standard nobody ever samples against real work is a document, not a standard. The gap between the two only becomes visible when something goes wrong, which is the most expensive possible moment to discover it.

The fifth is confusing a longer document with a better standard. A standard operating procedure padded out to twelve pages to look thorough is less likely to be followed than a one-page version that covers the same non-negotiable steps clearly, because the person doing the work under time pressure will skim or skip the long version and keep the short one open. Standardisation is about getting the method followed consistently, not about the document looking impressively thick and official on a shelf, and a shorter standard that's actually read beats a longer one that isn't, every time.

None of this needs to start as a company-wide initiative to be worth doing. The businesses that get standardisation right usually start with a single process, the one causing the most visible inconsistency right now, get that one genuinely working across every person and location involved, and only then move to the next. Trying to standardise everything in one pass tends to produce a stack of documents nobody has time to properly adopt, where starting narrow and finishing properly builds the habit, and the credibility, that makes the next process easier to standardise, and the one after that easier still, until it stops feeling like a separate project and becomes simply how the business runs.

Common questions.

What is process standardisation?

Process standardisation is making sure the same task gets done the same way regardless of who does it or where. That means one agreed method, written down, trained on, and checked, rather than each person or location quietly developing their own version over time.

Is process standardisation the same thing as writing an SOP?

Writing an SOP documents one version of a process. Standardisation is the wider job of getting everyone to actually follow that documented version, measuring whether they do, and updating it when a better method is found. An SOP without adoption and review is documentation, not standardisation.

Who should own process standardisation in a growing business?

One named owner per process family, not a single company-wide standardisation role in a smaller business. The person who runs the work day to day, or their direct manager, is usually better placed to keep a process current than a central quality team with no visibility into daily exceptions.

Does standardising a process mean removing all flexibility?

No. Good standardisation fixes the steps that must not vary, usually anything touching safety, compliance or a client-facing promise, and leaves everything else to judgement. Standardising every decision, including ones that genuinely depend on context, produces a process people route around rather than follow.

How do you know if a process actually needs standardising?

Ask two people who do the same job how they do it, separately, without letting them compare answers first. If the methods differ in a way that changes the outcome, quality, speed, compliance or the customer's experience, the process needs standardising. If they differ only in style with the same result, it probably doesn't.
