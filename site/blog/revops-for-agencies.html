Revenue operations

RevOps for agencies. The same operating discipline, running across every client account at once.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.9 November 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a revenue operations consultant for founder-led and agency teams. More on her background at the about section.

RevOps for an agency means connecting the systems behind new business, delivery and billing so every account reports against the same definitions, hours delivered against scope, response time, deliverable status, regardless of which account manager runs it. Without that, each account manager quietly builds their own version of the truth, and no two clients see numbers that mean the same thing.

The short answer. One shared definition of account health, not one per account manager.

Revenue operations started as a SaaS discipline: align the CRM, marketing automation and finance systems behind a single pipeline so forecasting and reporting stop depending on which spreadsheet someone happened to update that week. An agency has the same underlying problem, multiplied across every client it serves. New business runs in a CRM. Delivery runs in a project management tool. Billing runs in a finance system that rarely talks to either. Each account manager, left to their own devices, ends up stitching those three together by hand, every month, in their own slightly different way.

The pressure to fix that has grown for a reason that has nothing to do with agency efficiency and everything to do with how clients now buy. Sagefrog's 2026 B2B Marketing Mix Report found that the share of B2B companies using a hybrid model, combining an in-house marketing team with external agency support, rose from 36% in 2025 to 46% in 2026. A client running a hybrid model has their own in-house reporting standard sitting right next to whatever the agency hands them, and a hand-built, inconsistent monthly report stands out immediately against a client's own more disciplined internal dashboard. RevOps, for an agency, is what keeps that comparison from being embarrassing.

Agencies resist formalising this more than SaaS companies tend to, for an understandable reason: a lot of agency culture prizes the account manager's personal relationship with a client over any standardised process, and a shared reporting definition can feel like it's flattening what's genuinely a relationship-driven business. The two aren't actually in tension. A consistent account-health definition doesn't replace the relationship, it just means the numbers underneath that relationship hold up the moment a client asks a question the account manager can't answer from memory alone.

How it works in practice. The three systems agencies actually need to connect.

SystemWhat it ownsWhere it breaks without RevOps
CRM / new-business pipelineProspecting through to a signed contract, including the scope and pricing logic agreed along the wayThe handoff to delivery carries no shared record of what was actually promised
Project / delivery managementScope, hours and milestones per client, account by accountDelivery re-explains or re-negotiates scope because the sales team's notes never crossed over intact
Billing and financeRetainer versus project invoicing, utilisation, and account-level marginRevenue recognition and true account profitability become guesswork, hidden behind an agency-wide average

The CRM holds the clearest record of what a client was actually sold, the scope, the pricing assumptions, any discount or custom term agreed to close the deal. That detail is exactly what gets lost the moment a signed contract gets handed to delivery as a one-line project brief. A connected system, where the delivery tool references the same account record rather than starting a fresh one, keeps that context alive past the handoff, the same principle behind our revenue operations work generally, just pointed at the client-services handoff instead of the sales-to-customer-success one.

The project or delivery tool is where the day-to-day reality of an account actually lives, hours logged, milestones hit or missed, scope creep that's crept in without a change order. Left disconnected from the CRM, a renewal conversation six months later happens without sales ever seeing the delivery team's own notes about a client who's been quietly over-serviced or under-serviced relative to what they're paying.

Billing sits downstream of both and is usually the system that exposes the problem first, because it's where someone eventually has to reconcile what was promised against what was delivered against what was invoiced. An agency that can only see profitability at the whole-agency level, not broken out per account, is flying with one instrument covering for three, and a handful of loss-making retainers can sit hidden inside an average that still looks healthy.

One further friction point worth naming: a new-business cycle and a delivery cycle run on different clocks, and a connected system needs to respect that rather than force both onto one calendar. A deal might take three months to close and then run as an open-ended monthly retainer for two years, while delivery reports on a tighter weekly or bi-weekly rhythm throughout. The CRM should still hold the original scope and pricing logic as the reference point delivery reports against, even once the deal itself has long since closed and dropped out of the sales pipeline view.

What good looks like. A worked scenario on client reporting that didn't match itself.

An agency I worked with, illustrative rather than a named client, had every account manager building their own monthly client report by hand, pulling numbers from the CRM, the project tool and a shared time-tracking spreadsheet that wasn't quite kept up to date the same way by everyone. Two clients on the identical retainer tier could, and did, see different-looking reports in the same month, not because their results actually differed, but because each account manager had made their own small judgement calls about what counted as "billable hours" or "deliverables on track." When a client asked why their utilisation number didn't match the figure from the previous month's report, the honest answer, which nobody wanted to give out loud, was that the definition had quietly shifted because a different person had put the report together.

The fix was deliberately narrow rather than a full systems overhaul: define one account-health standard, hours delivered against scope, response time to client requests, and deliverable status, sourced from a single system of record rather than from whichever spreadsheet an account manager had open. That single definition then became what every client report pulled from, regardless of who was running the account. The decision rule worth keeping from that process: a shared definition doesn't need to be the most sophisticated version possible, it needs to be the same one everyone actually uses, because the sophistication saves nothing if half the team quietly reverts to their own method under deadline pressure.

Within the following quarter, the time account managers spent assembling monthly reports dropped by roughly half, since most of the numbers now pulled automatically from the shared system rather than being manually reconciled each month, and the agency fielded noticeably fewer client questions asking why a figure didn't match the one from the month before. The bigger shift was less visible on a spreadsheet: new account managers could be handed an existing client relationship and produce a report that looked identical to the one the previous account manager had been sending, because the definition lived in the system rather than in that person's head.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where agency RevOps quietly goes wrong.

The first pitfall is buying a reporting or BI tool before agreeing what "account health" actually means. A dashboard built on top of disagreement just displays the disagreement more clearly, with better charts, which is a worse outcome than the spreadsheet it replaced because it now looks authoritative.

The second is leaving the new-business CRM and the delivery tool fully disconnected, so a churn-risk signal, a client going quiet, scope tension building, sits visible only in delivery notes that sales never sees before a renewal conversation starts. The warning was there the whole time; it just never crossed the gap between the two systems.

The third is letting one person hold the reporting logic entirely in their head, the account manager who "just knows" how the numbers get pulled together. It works fine until that person is on leave, or leaves the agency, at which point the reporting doesn't just slow down, it becomes genuinely unclear how it was ever produced.

The fourth is measuring profitability only at the whole-agency level. An agency can look healthy on its combined numbers while carrying two or three accounts that have been quietly losing money for a year, propped up by everything else, with no account-level view to surface it until a wider slowdown makes the whole picture harder to ignore.

A fifth pitfall is rolling out a shared reporting standard without retiring the old, informal version alongside it. If the new system exists but the familiar spreadsheet still works and nobody's told account managers to stop using it, most will quietly keep the version they already trust, especially under deadline pressure, and the agency ends up maintaining two versions of the truth instead of one. The old method needs an explicit end date, not just a better alternative sitting next to it.

A sixth pitfall, specific to agencies rather than SaaS companies, is applying one reporting template to every client regardless of contract type. A fixed-scope project and an open-ended monthly retainer need genuinely different account-health measures, a project tracks against a finish line, a retainer tracks against a recurring value delivered, and forcing both into the same report format usually means the retainer clients get a report built for projects, one that emphasises completion percentage over the ongoing value a retainer is actually meant to demonstrate. Building two report templates, one for each contract type, sourced from the same underlying system, solves this without duplicating the underlying data.

Common questions.

What is RevOps for an agency?

RevOps for an agency means connecting the systems behind new business, delivery and billing so client-facing reporting stays consistent across every account, rather than being rebuilt by hand, differently, by each account manager every month. It's the same discipline SaaS companies apply to their own revenue engine, pointed at client accounts instead of a single pipeline.

Does a small agency need RevOps, or is that only for SaaS companies?

A small agency needs the discipline well before it needs a dedicated hire. Sagefrog's 2026 B2B Marketing Mix Report found that the share of B2B companies using a hybrid in-house-and-agency marketing model rose from 36% in 2025 to 46% in 2026, which means more agencies are being measured against an in-house team's own reporting standard, not just their own past invoices.

What's the difference between RevOps and traditional agency operations?

Traditional agency operations usually optimise each function on its own, new business chases its target, delivery manages its hours, finance reconciles invoices. RevOps treats those as one connected system with a shared definition of account health, so a number means the same thing whether it comes from the CRM, the project tool or the invoice.

Who owns RevOps in an agency that has no dedicated ops hire?

Usually whoever already owns the CRM or the project management tool, often an operations lead, a senior account director, or the founder in a smaller agency. The title matters less than having one named person responsible for the shared definitions, so they don't quietly diverge between account managers.

What's the first system an agency should fix?

The handoff between new business and delivery. It's the point where the most context gets lost, since the scope, promises and pricing logic a prospect heard in the sales process rarely survives the move into a delivery team's project tool intact, and every report built downstream inherits that gap.
