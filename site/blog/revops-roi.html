Revenue operations (RevOps)

RevOps ROI. What it actually measures.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.16 November 20268 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a revenue operations consultant for founder-led and SaaS teams. More on her background at the about section.

RevOps ROI is usually measured wrong, as a single growth multiple borrowed from someone else's case study. A case that holds up tracks four things before and after the investment: forecast accuracy, how long a new hire takes to ramp, win rate by segment, and the hours nobody has to spend reconciling three sets of numbers each week.

The short answer. Not a multiple, a measurement habit.

Search "RevOps ROI" and the numbers you'll find are wide enough to be close to useless. One report says businesses with a mature RevOps function grow at roughly a fifth faster than peers. Another puts it closer to three times faster. A widely quoted vendor-commissioned study lands on 3.3 times return over three years. None of these are necessarily wrong. They measure different things, at different company sizes, over different timeframes, and at least one of them was modelled on a composite enterprise with more than 1,500 people in its revenue organisation, which tells a twelve-person founder-led team almost nothing useful about its own situation.

The honest answer is that RevOps ROI isn't a multiple you borrow. It's four numbers tracked before and after the investment: forecast accuracy, ramp time for new hires, win rate by segment, and the hours someone no longer spends manually reconciling sales, marketing and finance figures before anyone trusts them. Measure those properly for two quarters and the case you end up with is built on your own business, not on a vendor's composite.

That matters because the businesses I see get this wrong usually do one of two things: they quote someone else's multiple to a board or a co-founder and then can't explain where their own number came from, or they give up measuring altogether because a single clean ROI figure feels impossible to produce. Both are avoidable with a shorter list of things actually worth tracking.

There's a reason the public multiples vary so widely. A growth-rate comparison, a cost-per-deal comparison and a composite ROI model are three different calculations wearing the same label, and each one is sensitive to how the underlying business defines "revenue function" in the first place. A company that counts only quota-carrying sales reps in that definition will show a different multiple to one that counts sales, marketing and customer success together, even if the actual work done is identical. None of that makes the public figures dishonest. It makes them specific to a calculation you didn't choose and can't inspect.

How it works in practice. Four numbers worth tracking before you spend.

Forrester's Total Economic Impact study of Salesloft, commissioned by the vendor and published in April 2025, modelled a composite $7 billion enterprise with more than 1,500 people across its revenue organisation and projected a 3.3 times return over three years, alongside a 12% improvement in close rates. The multiple is specific to that composite and shouldn't be quoted as a general RevOps benchmark. What's genuinely useful is the method behind it: Forrester measured rep productivity, reduced ramp time for new hires, fewer overlapping tools, and a measurable lift in win rate. Those four categories transfer to a business of any size. The size of the number does not.

Reconciliation hours are the number founders underrate most, because the cost is distributed across several people doing a little bit of manual stitching each, rather than concentrated in one obvious line item. A RevOps metrics practice that is working shows up first as that line item shrinking, often before win rate or ramp time have had time to move at all.

Baseline all four before a single system changes. The most common reason a RevOps business case falls apart six months in isn't that the investment failed, it's that nobody wrote down what "before" looked like, so there's nothing credible to compare "after" against.

It's also worth being honest that RevOps ROI compounds rather than landing all at once. The reconciliation hours usually drop first, within weeks, because that's a process change rather than a behaviour change. Win rate and ramp time move on a longer curve, tied to a full sales cycle and a full hiring cohort, and the return in year two of a properly run RevOps function is typically larger than year one simply because the data, the process and the people have had longer to settle into the new way of working. Judging the whole investment on a single quarter's numbers undersells it in one direction as often as it oversells it in the other.

What good looks like. A worked scenario on building the case properly.

An eighteen-person SaaS revenue team I worked with, illustrative rather than a named client, started from a familiar place: a forecast that moved by roughly 35% between what was promised to the board and what actually closed, a new sales hire who took close to five months to hit full quota, and three separate spreadsheets, one each for sales, marketing and customer success, that someone manually lined up every Friday before the weekly leadership update.

Before any tooling changed, we logged the baseline properly: two weeks of reconciliation time (just over six hours a week across two people), the actual ramp time of the last three hires, and the forecast variance from the previous two quarters pulled straight from the CRM rather than from memory. Only then did the RevOps work start, a shared data layer connecting the three functions, one forecast instead of three, and a standard first ninety days for new hires built around what the fastest-ramping rep had actually done differently.

The standard first ninety days itself was built from one source: a short interview with the one rep who had ramped fastest in the previous year, asked plainly what she had done in her first month that the others hadn't. The answer wasn't a training programme, it was that her manager had sat with her through her first five real forecast calls rather than leaving her to shadow a deck. That became the template, not a generic onboarding checklist bought off the shelf.

Two quarters later the forecast variance had narrowed to roughly 12%, reconciliation time had dropped to under an hour a week, and the next two hires reached full productivity in just over three months rather than five. None of those numbers came from a vendor study. They came from comparing the same four measurements against the baseline that had been written down before the work started.

The decision rule I give most founders at this stage: if you can't say what your reconciliation hours or forecast variance were three months ago, you are not ready to claim an ROI figure yet, whatever a case study says is possible. Write the baseline down first. The case builds itself from there.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where the RevOps business case falls apart.

The first pitfall is quoting someone else's multiple as if it were a forecast. A 3.3 times return modelled on a $7 billion enterprise says nothing reliable about what a twelve-person team should expect, and presenting it as a target sets up a comparison nobody can win.

The second is measuring from a baseline nobody can trust. A 2025 survey of RevOps professionals run by RevOps Co-op and BoostUp.ai found that 75% cite data inconsistencies as the most frustrating part of their tech stack. If the starting numbers are already unreliable, every "after" figure measured against them inherits the same problem, and the ROI case collapses the first time someone questions where the baseline came from.

The third is judging too early. Forecast accuracy and reconciliation hours move within weeks, but win rate and ramp time need a full sales cycle and at least one complete hiring cohort to mean anything. Calling the case a failure after six weeks because the headline metric hasn't moved yet usually just means the wrong metric was checked on the wrong timeline.

The fourth, and the one that costs the most over time, is leaving out the cost of not having RevOps at all. The real comparison isn't the cost of the function against zero. It's the cost of the function against the deals lost to handoff gaps between sales and customer success, the forecasts leadership has quietly stopped trusting, and the hours a founder or ops lead spends being the manual glue between systems that were never built to talk to each other. Most of that cost never appears on a spreadsheet, which is exactly why it gets left out of the case, and exactly why it should be the first thing written down.

The fifth is stopping the measurement the moment the business case gets approved. A RevOps function that proved its worth over two quarters and then stops tracking those four numbers loses the one thing that justified the investment in the first place: evidence. The measurement habit that built the case is the same one that should flag the quarter it starts slipping, long before anyone has to ask whether RevOps is still earning its budget.

Common questions.

What is RevOps ROI?

RevOps ROI is the measurable return on investing in shared revenue data, process and tooling across sales, marketing and customer success. Measured properly, it is not one multiple but four tracked numbers: forecast accuracy, new-hire ramp time, win rate by segment and the hours no longer spent manually reconciling reports.

Is the three-times-faster-growth RevOps statistic real?

Figures like this come from vendor-commissioned studies modelled on composite enterprises, not from independently observed outcomes across the market. Forrester's 2025 Total Economic Impact study of Salesloft, for example, modelled a composite $7 billion company with over 1,500 revenue staff to reach its 3.3 times return. The method behind the number is useful; the multiple itself rarely transfers to a smaller, founder-led business.

How long does it take to see ROI from a RevOps investment?

Allow at least two full quarters before judging it. Forecast accuracy and reconciliation time improve within weeks, but win rate and ramp time need a full sales cycle and at least one new-hire cohort to move through the system before the numbers mean anything.

What is the cheapest way to start measuring RevOps ROI?

Keep a two-week time log of hours spent manually reconciling sales, marketing and finance numbers before any investment starts. That single baseline, compared against the same task two quarters later, is often the clearest and cheapest evidence a founder actually trusts.

Should a small, founder-led business expect the same RevOps ROI as an enterprise?

No. Enterprise case studies measure return against a much larger base of reps, tools and reporting overhead, so the multiple looks bigger even when the underlying fix is the same. A smaller business should measure against its own baseline, not against a composite built to showcase a vendor's platform.

Does RevOps ROI include the cost of not having RevOps?

It should, and most business cases leave it out. The real comparison is the cost of the function against the cost of the status quo: deals lost to handoff gaps, forecasts leadership stops trusting, and the hours a founder or ops lead spends being the human glue between systems that do not talk to each other.
