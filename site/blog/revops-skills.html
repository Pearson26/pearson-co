The skills a RevOps leader needs. Five that actually matter.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.21 September 20269 min read

A RevOps leader needs five skills working together, not one held at a high level: CRM and systems fluency, data and reporting literacy, process design, cross-functional influence without formal authority, and the commercial judgement to turn a number into a decision. Most people are strong in one or two; the job breaks down wherever the other three are missing.

The short answer. A skill set, not a job title.

RevOps sits at the point where systems, data and three or four functions that do not naturally agree with each other all meet, and the skills the role needs follow directly from that position. It is a different question from what a RevOps analyst, manager or VP actually does day to day, which is about org structure and reporting lines. This is about the underlying capability a person needs to do any of those jobs well, at any level of seniority.

The mistake founder-led teams make most often is hiring for the skill that is easiest to test in an interview, usually systems fluency or data work, and assuming the rest will follow. It rarely does, because the harder skills, process design and cross-functional influence in particular, only show up once someone is actually in the seat, trying to get two teams to agree on something neither one is required to agree to. It is also the reason a strong RevOps candidate can interview brilliantly and still struggle in the role: most interview processes are built to test the two skills that are easiest to demonstrate on a call, not the three that actually determine whether the function works six months in.

The five skills. And where each one actually shows up.

CRM and systems fluency. Not development, configuration. A RevOps leader needs to be comfortable building a workflow, adjusting a pipeline stage, or working out why a field is not populating correctly, inside the CRM's own tools rather than needing a developer for routine changes. This is the most commonly tested skill in hiring, and the one with the lowest ceiling on how much it alone can achieve, since a perfectly configured CRM full of definitions nobody trusts still produces bad decisions.

Data and reporting literacy. The ability to build a report that survives scrutiny, and just as importantly, to know when a number is wrong before someone in a leadership meeting spots it first. This overlaps closely with what makes a sales pipeline report trustworthy rather than merely complete: knowing which fields actually matter, and which ones just look thorough.

Process design. The instinct to look at a messy, informal way of working, an approval that happens over email, a handoff nobody owns, and see the underlying steps clearly enough to redesign them. This is the same skill a good process map depends on, applied specifically to revenue-facing workflows: lead routing, deal handoffs, renewal triggers. Someone strong here tends to be uncomfortable with an undocumented process, which is a genuinely useful discomfort in this role.

Cross-functional influence. Getting sales, marketing, customer success and sometimes finance to agree on one shared definition, what counts as a qualified lead, when a deal actually moves stage, what "active customer" means, usually with no formal authority to make any of them comply. This is the skill hiring managers test for least and need most. A RevOps leader without it can build a technically correct system that three different teams quietly work around because nobody asked them what would actually make it usable for their job.

Commercial judgement. The ability to translate a metric into a decision a leader can act on, rather than presenting a dashboard and calling the job done. This is closer to a sales or finance instinct than a technical one: knowing that a weakening conversion rate on a shrinking lead volume is a different problem from the same rate on a growing one, and being able to say which lever is actually worth pulling first.

Lined up together, the five skills and what happens when one is missing are easier to see as a set than as five separate paragraphs.

What good looks like. Reasonably capable across all five, not excellent in one.

The strongest RevOps hires are not the ones who score highest on any single skill in an interview. They are the ones who are competent across all five at once, because the job's value comes from the combination, not from depth in one lane. A brilliant systems person with no cross-functional influence builds workflows other teams quietly bypass. A sharp analyst with no process-design instinct produces dashboards that describe a mess accurately without ever fixing it. A confident stakeholder manager with no data literacy talks a good game in meetings and cannot back it up when someone asks a specific question about the number on the screen.

This is also why the generalist-first hiring sequence, already the standard pattern for building out a RevOps team's structure, applies just as much to the individual skill profile as it does to headcount. A founder-led team's first RevOps hire needs to be genuinely useful across all five skills at a moderate level, not outstanding in the one that happens to match whatever the founder finds most interesting to test for. Specialists earn their place once a generalist has already proven where the function's actual bottleneck sits.

The Revenue Operations Alliance's RevOps Skills Survey 2026 points to the same shift from a different angle: as AI tools increasingly absorb the routine data pulls and report-building that used to fill a RevOps hire's week, the survey found the skills employers now weight more heavily are the ones that were always hardest to automate, cross-functional influence and the commercial judgement to decide what a number actually means, rather than the mechanical work of producing it.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where a skills mismatch actually breaks things.

Hiring a systems administrator and calling it RevOps. Someone who can build any workflow asked of them, but has no process-design instinct of their own, ends up automating whatever process already exists, mess included, rather than fixing it first.

Skipping the influence test entirely. Most interview processes test data and systems skills directly, through a case study or a technical question, and test cross-functional influence only indirectly, through a general "tell me about a time" question that is easy to answer well without actually having the skill.

Treating commercial judgement as something seniority automatically brings. A junior hire with strong natural judgement about which metric actually matters this quarter is more useful than a senior one who reports everything with equal weight and lets someone else decide what it means.

Assuming the skill profile stays fixed as the team grows. What a solo RevOps hire needs at a ten-person startup, breadth across all five at a workable level, is not what a RevOps leader managing three specialists at a two hundred-person company needs, where the leader's own cross-functional influence and commercial judgement matter more than staying hands-on in the CRM. Hiring the second RevOps person with the same brief used for the first is a common way a growing team ends up with two generalists and no depth anywhere.

How Lauren would hire for it. The question I actually ask.

I stopped asking RevOps candidates to walk me through a dashboard build some time ago, because almost everyone competent enough to be in the room can do that adequately. Take a hypothetical, but representative, situation built to reflect the kind of conversation I actually have rather than one named client: a mid-sized SaaS business had hired a technically excellent RevOps lead, someone who could build anything in the CRM and produce a clean report inside a day. Nine months in, sales and marketing were still arguing about what counted as a qualified lead, using two different numbers in two different weekly meetings, because nobody with the standing to force a shared definition had actually gone and sat with both teams to work one out.

What I ask instead now is simpler: describe a time you got two teams that disagreed to accept one shared definition of something, and what you actually said in the room to get there. The answer tells me more about whether someone will succeed in a RevOps seat than any amount of systems or dashboard testing, because the technical work is genuinely learnable on the job. The ability to sit between two functions that do not report to you and get them to agree on one number is not something most people pick up by month six if it was not there to begin with.

My rule: hire for the ability to get two teams to agree on one definition, not for tool fluency alone. Systems skills close the gap between a decision and its execution. Influence and judgement are what get the decision made in the first place, and they are far harder to teach after the fact. If a candidate cannot describe a specific moment they did this, not a general philosophy about collaboration but an actual room, an actual disagreement and what they said, that is the gap to plan for, not the CRM certification on their CV.

Common questions.

What are the core skills a RevOps leader needs?

Five: CRM and systems fluency, data and reporting literacy, process design, cross-functional influence, and commercial judgement. Most RevOps hires are strong in one or two of these and weak in the rest, which is usually where a RevOps function stalls rather than in any single missing skill.

Is a data analyst background enough to become a good RevOps leader?

It covers one of the five skills well but rarely all of them. A strong analytical background without cross-functional influence often produces accurate dashboards nobody acts on, because getting sales, marketing and finance to agree on shared definitions is a different skill from building the report itself.

Do you need to be technical to work in RevOps?

You need enough systems fluency to configure a CRM workflow or read a report's underlying logic, not necessarily to write code. Most day-to-day RevOps work happens inside a CRM's own configuration tools, and the technical bar is lower than people expect, while the cross-functional and judgement bar is higher.

What is the single hardest RevOps skill to hire for?

Cross-functional influence without formal authority. A RevOps hire often has to get sales, marketing and customer success to agree on one shared definition of a lead or a stage, with no power to force any of them to comply. Systems and data skills can be taught faster than the judgement to navigate that without becoming everyone's least favourite meeting.

Should a first RevOps hire be a generalist or a specialist?

A generalist, almost always. A specialist in one of the five skills, usually data or systems, is only useful once the other four are already covered by someone else in the business. A founder-led team hiring its first RevOps person needs someone reasonably capable across all five, not excellent in one.
