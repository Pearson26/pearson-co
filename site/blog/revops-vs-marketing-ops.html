Revenue Operations (RevOps)

RevOps vs marketing ops. Where the line actually sits.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.31 August 20267 min read

Marketing operations manages the systems and process inside the marketing team alone: campaigns, lead scoring, attribution and the martech stack. RevOps sits above all of that, aligning marketing, sales and customer success around one connected process, one data model and one forecast. Most companies need marketing ops first and RevOps once a second revenue function exists.

What to look for. Where the split actually sits.

RevOps vs marketing ops confuses people because the two often live inside the same person for the first few years of a company's life. Marketing operations is the function that runs the systems and process inside marketing alone: the martech stack, lead scoring, campaign builds, list hygiene, attribution reporting. It answers to the marketing team, and its job is marketing's own productivity. Revenue operations sits a level above that. It aligns marketing, sales and customer success around one connected process, one shared data model and one forecast, and it typically reports to a Chief Revenue Officer or directly to the founder, not to the head of marketing.

The practical test is scope. If the question is "how do we route leads inside HubSpot" or "why is our email attribution wrong", that is marketing operations. If the question is "why does marketing say we generated 200 qualified leads this month and sales says it only received 90", that is a RevOps problem, because it sits in the handoff between two functions and neither team owns the whole picture on its own. Marketing ops fixes systems within a department. RevOps fixes the seams between departments.

The confusion gets worse because a lot of RevOps job descriptions grew out of marketing operations roles. Someone hired three years ago to manage a marketing automation platform ends up, as the company adds a sales team and a customer success function, inheriting responsibility for the CRM, the forecast and the renewal data too, and their title eventually catches up with what they are actually doing. That career path is common, but it means plenty of companies have someone doing genuine RevOps work under a marketing ops title, or the reverse: someone with a RevOps title who has never actually been given authority over anything outside marketing's own tools.

The two rows worth paying attention to are "reports to" and "fixes", because they explain most of the organisational disputes that come up when a company tries to draw this line for the first time. A marketing ops hire, however senior, cannot mandate a change in how the sales team logs a deal stage; that is outside their reporting line and outside their actual authority, whatever their title says. A RevOps hire can, precisely because their remit was built to cross that boundary from day one. Getting this backwards, giving a marketing-ops-scoped person a RevOps title without the cross-functional authority to match, is one of the more common reasons a first RevOps hire quietly fails to change anything.

Data quality is where the two functions overlap most visibly, and it is worth being honest about how common the problem still is. RevOps Coop's 2025 State of RevOps Survey found that 99 percent of respondents reported dealing with some form of technical data issue, spanning duplicate records, broken integrations and inconsistent field definitions across systems. A marketing ops team can keep its own database clean. Whether that clean data stays clean once it crosses into the CRM, and whether sales and customer success are entering data against the same definitions marketing used, is a RevOps question, not a marketing ops one.

Tooling ownership is another place the two roles get tangled. A marketing ops person choosing a new marketing automation tool only needs to think about how it serves marketing's own workflows. A RevOps person evaluating the same purchase has to ask a wider question: does this tool's data model integrate cleanly with the CRM, and will it create a fifth source of truth that someone has to reconcile by hand every month. That is why RevOps hires are usually given a seat in any tech-stack decision that touches lead or customer data, even tools that will sit inside marketing day to day, while a marketing ops role is not typically consulted on a change to the sales team's CRM configuration.

Which to choose and when. Team size and stage.

An early-stage company with one revenue-facing motion, usually a founder or a small team both marketing and selling, does not need a RevOps function yet. What it needs is marketing ops discipline: one martech stack, not three overlapping tools bought by different people; consistent lead scoring; and someone who owns the data rather than everyone touching it a little. Hiring a RevOps title at this stage tends to produce a marketing ops job with an inflated name, because there is no second function yet for that person to align anything with.

The trigger for RevOps is structural, not a headcount number. It is the point at which a second revenue-facing function exists, a dedicated sales team separate from marketing, or a customer success function handling renewals, and that function starts keeping its own version of the truth. Once two teams each have a number for "how many qualified opportunities do we have this month" and the numbers do not match, the company has a RevOps problem whether or not anyone has given it that name yet. Waiting until the mismatch becomes a board-level embarrassment is the expensive way to notice.

Companies that scale past this point without ever building a RevOps layer tend to end up with three or four disconnected reporting views: a marketing dashboard, a sales pipeline view, a customer success renewal tracker, and a founder trying to reconcile all three by hand before every leadership meeting. That reconciliation work is, functionally, the RevOps job already being done badly by whoever draws the short straw each month. Naming it and giving one person or a small team the mandate to own it properly is usually cheaper than the hours it was already quietly costing.

The most common mistake I see at this stage is not skipping RevOps altogether, it is bolting the title onto the existing marketing ops hire without changing their actual authority. Giving someone a RevOps job title while leaving them reporting into the CMO, with no standing to require sales to change a stage definition, produces a person who is nominally responsible for cross-functional alignment but structurally unable to deliver it. If the reporting line does not change, the role has not actually changed either, whatever the org chart says.

How Lauren would decide. The practical test.

I use one question with founders who ask me whether they need marketing ops or RevOps: how many teams produce a number about revenue, and do those numbers currently agree? If the answer is one team, build marketing ops discipline and move on. If the answer is two or more and the numbers already disagree, that gap is the actual job description, not a hypothetical one.

I worked with an early-growth B2B SaaS company where a marketing operations manager had spent two years building a genuinely well-run HubSpot instance: clean segmentation, tight scoring, solid attribution. The problem only surfaced once the company hired its first dedicated sales team of four. Sales ran its pipeline in a separate CRM view with its own stage definitions, and within one quarter, marketing was reporting a lead-to-opportunity conversion rate that sales' own numbers flatly contradicted. Neither team was lying or careless; they were simply measuring against different definitions of "qualified" that had never been reconciled, because no one's job was to reconcile them. We brought in a fractional RevOps lead for one quarter, rebuilt the shared pipeline definitions across both systems, and gave that person, not marketing, ownership of the combined forecast. The two numbers converged within six weeks, not because either team's underlying data got better, but because both teams were finally being measured against the same definitions.

My rule: do not hire a RevOps lead before you have at least two functions producing revenue numbers that need reconciling. One tool, one team, does not need revenue operations, it needs someone genuinely good at running that one tool well. RevOps earns its cost the moment a second team's numbers enter the picture and someone has to decide which version of the truth the board actually sees.

Common questions.

What is the difference between RevOps and marketing ops?

Marketing operations manages the systems and process inside the marketing team alone, things like the marketing automation platform, lead scoring and campaign reporting. Revenue operations sits above that, aligning marketing, sales and customer success around one connected process, one data model and one forecast, and usually reports to a CRO or the founder rather than to marketing.

Does a small business need RevOps or just marketing ops?

A small business with one revenue-facing function, usually marketing feeding a founder who also sells, typically only needs marketing ops discipline: a clean stack and clear lead scoring. RevOps earns its keep once a second function exists, a dedicated sales team or customer success, and the numbers between two teams stop agreeing.

Who does RevOps report to compared with marketing ops?

Marketing operations typically reports to the head of marketing or CMO, since its mandate is marketing's own productivity. Revenue operations typically reports to a Chief Revenue Officer, the CEO or the founder directly, because its mandate spans departments that no single functional head can set standards for on their own.

Can one person do both marketing ops and RevOps?

Yes, and in founder-led and early-stage companies this is the norm rather than the exception. The risk is not the workload, it is authority: that one person needs a mandate to set standards across sales and customer success too, not just inside marketing, or the RevOps half of the job never actually happens.

What tools does each function typically own?

Marketing operations typically owns the marketing automation platform, forms, landing pages, lead scoring rules and attribution reporting. Revenue operations typically owns the CRM's data model and pipeline stages, the handoff definitions between systems, and the reporting layer that pulls from marketing, sales and customer success into one view.

When should a company hire its first RevOps lead?

Once two revenue-facing functions exist and each one is reporting a different version of the same number, marketing's qualified lead count against sales' received lead count, for example. Before that point, a strong marketing operations hire, or a founder disciplined about one shared spreadsheet, usually covers the need without a dedicated RevOps role.
