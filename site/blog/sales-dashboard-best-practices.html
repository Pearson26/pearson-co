Sales reporting, dashboards & forecasting

Sales dashboard best practices. The ones that actually hold up.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.25 November 20267 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a CRM and revenue operations consultant for founder-led and SaaS teams. More on her background at the about section.

The sales dashboards that keep getting used share a short list of habits: one named owner, a refresh cadence matched to the decision it informs, a visible calculation behind every number, and a hard limit on how much fits the screen. Build around those and most of the usual dashboard problems never get the chance to start.

Why this matters. Most dashboards die from clutter, not from bad design.

I'm Lauren Pearson, and I've built and audited enough sales dashboards now to notice a pattern that has nothing to do with the tool underneath them. The dashboards that get opened every week and the ones that quietly stop getting opened after a few months rarely differ on design polish or which platform they're built in. They differ on a handful of operating habits that either got set at the start or never did.

Salesforce's State of Sales research, fielded in a survey of 7,775 sales professionals in August and September 2022, found that 66% of sales reps said they felt overwhelmed by too many tools. That survey is a few years old now, but the underlying dynamic has not reversed. Most teams have added software and metrics since, not removed them, and a sales dashboard that keeps growing without anyone pruning it is simply that same tool-sprawl problem, compressed onto one page. A dashboard with twenty-three tiles is not more informative than one with six. It is just slower to read, which in practice means it gets read less.

The list below is not about which charting library to use or which CRM has the better reporting layer. It is about the operating discipline that keeps a dashboard useful past its first month, because that is almost always where the real failure happens.

The list, with how to apply each. Eight habits, not eight features.

  1. Build around one decision, not a department. Before adding a panel, name the specific decision it exists to inform, a founder deciding whether to hire another rep, a sales lead deciding which deals need help this week. A dashboard built for "the sales team" in general tends to answer nobody's actual question well. One built around a decision has a natural limit on what belongs on it.
  2. Match the refresh cadence to how fast the decision actually changes. A weekly pipeline review does not need a live-updating feed; it needs numbers that were accurate as of Monday morning. Reserve real-time or near-real-time refresh for the handful of metrics where a stale view genuinely risks a bad call within the day, and leave everything else on the simplest cadence that still feels current.
  3. Show the calculation, not just the output number. A win rate on its own invites an argument about whether it is right. A win rate with a visible note of what counts as "won" and what the denominator includes settles that argument before it starts, and it is the difference between a number people trust and one they quietly second-guess.
  4. Give every dashboard exactly one owner. Not a team, one named person responsible for the definitions staying current and the panels staying accurate. Shared ownership of a dashboard tends to mean nobody notices when a field changes meaning in the CRM until someone in a meeting asks a question the numbers can no longer answer correctly.
  5. Segment by role before you segment by metric. A rep, a sales lead and a founder are three different audiences with three different questions, and a single dashboard trying to serve all three usually ends up serving none of them well. Build the rep view around their own pipeline, the leadership view around trend and risk, and resist merging them back into one page for the sake of tidiness.
  6. Put a retirement date on every panel. When a metric gets added for a specific, time-bound reason, a product launch, a pricing test, write down when it should be reviewed for removal. Panels that go on forever because nobody decided to take them off are the single biggest reason dashboards grow past the point anyone can read them at a glance.
  7. Define the metric in writing before anyone builds the query. What counts as a qualified lead, which stage a deal has to reach to count as pipeline, whether a renewal counts as new revenue. Agree this on paper first. Building the visual layer before the definitions are settled is the most common reason a new dashboard gets disputed the first time someone looks closely at it.
  8. Cap it. If it does not fit one screen, something comes off. Scrolling defeats the entire point of a dashboard, which is letting someone see the state of things in the time it takes to glance at a screen. A hard cap forces the harder, more useful conversation about which four to eight numbers actually matter, instead of letting the list grow by default.

Where teams go wrong. The pattern behind most dashboard failures.

Picture an eight-person agency sales team whose main pipeline dashboard had grown, over about a year, from four panels to twenty-three. Nobody had decided to add that much. Each tile had arrived for a reasonable reason at the time, a client asked for a view, a new hire wanted their own breakdown, a one-off report got pinned permanently instead of being archived once the question it answered was settled. By the time anyone looked at the dashboard as a whole, most of the team had quietly stopped opening it and gone back to asking their sales lead questions directly instead.

The fix was not a redesign. It was running the eight habits above against the existing panels: naming the one decision each surviving panel needed to serve, assigning a single owner, and agreeing in writing what each remaining metric actually counted. Fifteen of the twenty-three panels did not survive that process, not because they were wrong, but because nobody could say which decision they were there to support. What was left fit one screen. The team started opening the dashboard again within the first few weeks, not because it looked better, but because it answered a question faster than asking a person did.

That is the real test worth applying to any sales dashboard, new or existing. Not whether it looks complete, but whether someone can look at it for ten seconds and know what to do next. A dashboard that needs explaining in the meeting it was meant to replace has already failed at its one job, however much work went into building it.

Common questions.

What is the single most important sales dashboard best practice?

Giving the dashboard one owner. A dashboard with no named owner drifts: a metric definition changes in one system, a panel breaks quietly, and nobody notices until someone in a meeting asks a question the numbers can no longer answer. Every other practice on this list is easier once one person is accountable for the page staying correct.

How many metrics should a sales dashboard show?

As few as the decision needs, usually somewhere between four and eight. A dashboard that needs scrolling has already failed its main job, which is letting someone see the state of the pipeline in the time it takes to glance at a screen.

Should a sales dashboard update in real time?

Only for the specific numbers where a stale view would cause a bad decision within the day, a live deal desk view during a renewal push, for example. Most pipeline and forecasting metrics are reviewed weekly or monthly, so a daily or even real-time refresh on them adds engineering cost without adding a better decision.

Why do sales dashboards stop being used after a few months?

Usually because panels were added faster than they were removed. Each new request felt reasonable on its own, but nobody owned deciding what came off, so the page grew past the point where anyone could read it at a glance and people quietly stopped opening it.

What should come before building a sales dashboard?

A written definition of each metric the dashboard will show: what counts as a qualified lead, which stage a deal has to reach to count as pipeline, and so on. Building the visual layer before agreeing those definitions is the single most common reason a dashboard gets disputed in its first review.
