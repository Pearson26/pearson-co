Sales reporting, dashboards & forecasting

Sales forecasting best practices. What actually holds up under a real pipeline review.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.28 October 20267 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a CRM and RevOps consultant for founder-led teams. More on her background at the about section.

Sales forecasting best practice means picking one method and sticking with it for a full cycle, weighting the pipeline by real stage-specific win rates rather than gut feel, reviewing the number on a fixed weekly cadence, and keeping commit and best case as two separate figures with one named owner for the roll-up.

Why this matters. Most forecasts fail on process, not prediction.

A sales forecast is only as good as the habits behind it. Founders and sales leaders often treat a missed forecast as a prediction problem, the market shifted, a deal went quiet, when in most cases the real issue sits earlier: the data feeding the number was never clean enough to trust, or the process for building it changed every month. Salesforce's State of Sales report, which surveyed 5,500 sales professionals between March and April 2024, found that only 35% completely trusted their own organisation's data accuracy. That is the real starting point for forecasting best practice: before the method matters, the inputs have to be ones people actually believe.

The list below is not a theoretical framework. It is what tends to separate a forecast a board can plan around from one that gets revised every week for reasons nobody can explain. None of the nine practices below requires new software or a bigger team. Most of them are habits a sales operations function can put in place inside a single quarter, which is usually faster than most founders expect once they stop treating a bad forecast as a market problem.

The list, with how to apply each. Nine practices, in the order they usually pay off.

1. Pick one method and run it for a full cycle

Whether you forecast bottom-up from individual deals or top-down from historical conversion rates, as covered in our comparison of the two approaches, switching methods mid-quarter because last month's number was wrong destroys the one thing that makes any method useful: a consistent baseline to measure drift against.

2. Weight the pipeline by stage-specific win rates, not gut feel

A deal at proposal stage does not close at the same rate as a deal at discovery, and that rate is a number you can calculate from your own closed-won history rather than estimate. Replace "I feel good about this one" with the actual historical close rate for that stage, updated at least twice a year as the sales motion changes.

3. Review the forecast on a fixed weekly cadence

The same day, the same attendees, every week. An irregular or monthly review means slippage gets noticed only once it is too late to do anything about the quarter. A fixed cadence is also what makes a live sales dashboard worth building: the number is only as current as the habit of actually looking at it.

4. Separate commit from best case, and track both over time

Commit is the number you would put your own bonus against. Best case is every deal that could plausibly close if the quarter breaks your way. Collapsing them into a single figure, which happens more often than most sales leaders admit, is how a board ends up planning headcount against a number that was always aspirational.

5. Audit the CRM data the forecast is built on

Stale close dates, duplicate deals and stages that were never actually updated are the most common reason a forecast swings wildly from one review to the next. A quarterly pipeline hygiene pass, checking that every open deal has a realistic close date and an owner who can speak to its status, catches most of this before it reaches the forecast.

6. Build in a known slippage rate rather than ignoring it

Deals pushed from this quarter to next are normal, not a failure. What matters is whether you track your own historical slippage rate and apply it to the forecast, rather than assuming this quarter will be the one where nothing moves.

7. Use a range, not a single number, for anything beyond six weeks out

A single point estimate for a quarter that has barely started implies a precision the data cannot support. A range, built from your historical forecast accuracy at that distance, is more honest and, over time, better trusted once it is consistently right about where reality lands.

8. Make one person accountable for the roll-up number

When every rep's individual forecast simply gets summed with no adjustment, optimism compounds. One named owner, usually in sales operations or revenue leadership, applying a consistent discount for historical over-forecasting, produces a far steadier number than an unadjusted sum of eight individually hopeful ones.

9. Close the loop: compare forecast to actual, every quarter, in writing

Few teams actually write down how far off last quarter's forecast was and why. Doing this consistently is what turns forecasting from guesswork into a process that gets measurably more accurate each cycle, because the same mistakes get caught before they repeat. A short written note, three or four lines, naming which deals moved the number and why the gap happened, is usually enough; the discipline of writing it matters more than its length.

These nine practices reinforce each other rather than standing alone. A fixed weekly review is far less useful without clean CRM data feeding it, and stage-weighted probabilities mean little if nobody is accountable for applying a consistent slippage discount to the roll-up. Teams that try to adopt just one or two in isolation, usually the review cadence, since it's the easiest to schedule, often find the forecast still swings wildly because the underlying data and ownership gaps were never addressed.

Where teams go wrong. A forecast built on hope, not history.

A founder-led B2B SaaS team I worked with, illustrative rather than a named result, had a forecast that swung by 30 to 40% between consecutive weekly reviews for most of a year. The cause was not a volatile market. Reps were self-rating every deal's likelihood on instinct, with no shared definition of what "80% likely" actually meant, and the sales leader was simply summing those numbers without adjustment. Replacing self-rated probability with stage-specific historical win rates, and adding a single named owner for the roll-up who applied a consistent slippage discount, settled the swing to within 10% inside two quarters. Nothing about the market changed. The process that turned individual guesses into one number did.

The opposite failure is just as common: forecasting with so much process rigour, elaborate scoring models, multiple approval layers, that nobody can explain the number in a sentence when a board member asks where it came from. The decision rule that holds up in practice: a forecast should be rigorous enough to be trusted and simple enough that the person presenting it can explain, off the top of their head, exactly which inputs moved it this week.

Common questions.

What is the single most important sales forecasting best practice?

Picking one forecasting method, sticking to it for a full quarter, and tracking how far it drifted from the actual close. Teams that swap methods every time a forecast misses never build the data needed to trust any of them.

How often should a sales forecast be reviewed?

Weekly, on a fixed day, with the same people in the room each time. A monthly or ad hoc review catches slippage too late to do anything useful about it, and a fixed cadence is what makes the number trustworthy rather than a guess refreshed under pressure.

Should commit and best case be the same number?

No. Commit is what you would bet your own bonus on closing; best case is what closes if every stalled deal moves your way. Collapsing the two into one figure is one of the most common ways forecasts mislead a board or an investor.

How accurate should a sales forecast actually be?

There is no universal benchmark, and reported figures vary widely by source and methodology, but a forecast that is consistently off by more than 15 to 20% in either direction is usually a data or process problem, not bad luck, and worth investigating rather than accepting.

Does CRM data quality really affect forecast accuracy?

Yes, more than most teams assume. Salesforce's 2024 State of Sales report, surveying 5,500 sales professionals, found only 35% completely trusted their organisation's data accuracy, and a forecast built on stage dates and amounts nobody trusts will always carry that same doubt forward.

Who should own the final forecast number?

One named person, usually sales operations or a revenue leader, who rolls up the reps' individual numbers, applies a consistent slippage adjustment, and is accountable for explaining the gap when actuals land. A forecast nobody owns is a forecast nobody can improve.
