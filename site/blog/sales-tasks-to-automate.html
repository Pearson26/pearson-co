CRM automation & workflow

Sales tasks worth automating. The ones that actually save time.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.3 August 20267 min read

The sales tasks worth automating first are repetitive, rule-based, and currently done inconsistently by different people: lead routing, follow-up reminders, meeting scheduling, quote generation and pipeline data entry. Automate those before anything more ambitious. They remove admin time without touching a single judgement call a rep actually needs to make.

I'm Lauren Pearson, and when a founder asks me which sales tasks to automate first, they are usually picturing something clever, an AI assistant that writes follow-up emails, a bot that scores leads. The tasks that save the most time in practice are far more boring than that. They are the ten below, and most of them can be switched on inside your existing CRM this month, without buying anything new.

Why this matters. The maths behind the list.

Salesforce's 2026 State of Sales report found that reps spend just 40% of their week actively selling. Of the time that goes elsewhere, manual data entry alone accounts for 22% and creating quotes another 11%, ahead of prospecting itself. That is not a productivity problem you fix by asking reps to work harder. It is a task-design problem, and most of the tasks eating that time are exactly the kind a workflow rule handles without complaint.

The tasks below are not ranked by how impressive they sound. They are ranked by how often I see them still being done by hand in a founder-led sales team, and how little judgement each one genuinely requires once you look closely at it.

The list, with how to apply each. Ten tasks, one line on how.

  1. Lead routing and assignment. A rule based on territory, deal size or simple round-robin gets a new lead in front of a named rep within minutes, instead of sitting in a shared inbox until someone notices it. Every major CRM builds this in.
  2. Follow-up reminders and cadences. A task or sequence fires automatically when a lead goes quiet for a set number of days, catching the single most common and most preventable reason a deal dies: nobody followed up.
  3. Meeting scheduling. A scheduling link removes the email back-and-forth of finding a slot. For inbound enquiries especially, this alone can shave a day or more off the time between first contact and a booked call.
  4. Call and email logging. Native CRM integrations with your phone and inbox log activity automatically, so a rep is not pasting call notes into a record by hand after every conversation.
  5. Quote and proposal generation. A templated builder pulls product and pricing data straight from the deal record, rather than a rep retyping a proposal from a blank document each time.
  6. Contract and e-signature reminders. An automatic nudge fires when a contract has sat unsigned for a set number of days, instead of a rep having to remember which of fifteen open deals needs chasing today.
  7. Deal stage and field updates. A trigger, an email opened, a contract signed, a payment received, moves the stage or updates the field automatically, keeping the pipeline accurate without five manual edits per deal.
  8. Renewal and expansion reminders. A task fires a set number of days before a contract's renewal date, so a renewal conversation starts with enough runway rather than in the final week.
  9. Handoff notifications to customer success. The moment a deal closes won, an automatic notification and a task hand the account to whoever owns onboarding, with the context already attached rather than relayed from memory in a hallway conversation.
  10. Pipeline reporting refresh. A live dashboard pulling straight from the CRM replaces the Friday export, so the numbers a founder checks on Monday are current rather than four days stale.

Notice what none of these ten do: none of them decide a discount, write a negotiation email, or judge whether a prospect is genuinely ready to buy. That line matters, and it is worth naming plainly. I generally tell clients not to automate quote generation until pricing is standardised across the team. I have watched a ten-person agency switch on automated proposal generation before agreeing internally on discount bands, and every automatically generated quote still needed manual correction before it went out, which was slower than the manual process it replaced. Automate the mechanics once the underlying decision is settled, not before.

Where teams go wrong. Common mistakes with this list.

The first mistake is trying to switch on all ten at once. Pick the two or three causing the most friction today, prove they work and get adopted, then move to the next two or three. A team that automates everything in a single sprint usually cannot tell which change actually helped when something later goes wrong.

The second is automating a step that still needs a person's judgement wrapped inside it, discount approval hidden inside "quote generation," for instance. Split the mechanical part from the judgement part, and automate only the mechanical half.

The third is reminder overload. Automating a nudge for every single task on this list at once creates so many notifications that reps start ignoring the CRM's alerts altogether, including the two or three that actually matter. Turn off any reminder a rep has ignored for a few weeks running rather than assuming more automation always means more discipline.

The fourth is forgetting the field team. If part of your sales team works mostly from a phone between site visits, an automation that only surfaces cleanly on desktop will get quietly worked around within a month. Check that whatever you switch on works properly in the CRM's mobile app before calling it live.

Start with whichever task on this list you are personally still doing by hand most days. That is usually the clearest signal of where the automation will actually get used, rather than where it looks best in a demo. Common questions.

Which sales task should I automate first?

Whichever one currently causes the most friction, but for most founder-led teams that is lead routing or follow-up reminders, since both remove a delay that directly costs deals and neither needs historical data to work well from day one.

Can I automate quote and proposal generation without a specialised tool?

Yes, for a straightforward pricing structure. Most CRMs, including HubSpot, Pipedrive and Salesforce, include native quote or document generation that pulls product and pricing data directly from the deal record. A dedicated tool like PandaDoc or Proposify earns its cost once you need e-signature tracking or more complex approval workflows built in.

Will automating sales tasks replace the need for reps to update the CRM manually?

Mostly, for the tasks on this list, but not entirely. Automation removes repetitive, rule-based updates, a stage changing when a contract is signed, a task being created when a lead goes quiet, but a rep still needs to log the substance of a call or a negotiation, since that context is not something a workflow rule can infer.

How do I stop automated reminders from becoming noise?

Limit reminders to tasks with a clear owner and a clear next action, and turn off any that a rep has ignored for more than a few weeks running. A reminder nobody acts on is not a productivity tool, it is background noise that trains people to ignore the CRM's notifications altogether, including the ones that matter.

Is meeting scheduling worth automating for a very small sales team?

Usually yes, even for a team of one or two. A scheduling link removes several email exchanges per booked meeting, and the time saved compounds quickly once you count every inbound enquiry across a month, regardless of how few reps are handling them.
