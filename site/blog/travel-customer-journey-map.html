Customer Journey Mapping

A travel customer journey map. Five stages, one non-linear path.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.31 August 20267 min read

A travel customer journey map plots the traveller's path through five stages: dreaming, planning, booking, experiencing and sharing, each with its own channels, content needs and drop-off risk. It has to be built differently from a standard B2B map because the journey is not linear. Over 40 percent of travellers move back and forth between dreaming and planning before ever reaching booking.

The short answer. Five stages, not four.

A travel customer journey map has to work harder than a typical B2B version because travel buying genuinely does not move in a straight line. Google, working with the travel research firm Phocuswright, mapped the journey into five stages: dreaming, when someone starts imagining a getaway with no fixed destination yet; planning, once a destination is chosen and dates, options and prices are being compared; booking, the purchase itself; experiencing, the trip; and sharing, the reviews, photos and word of mouth that follow and feed the next traveller's dreaming stage. Most generic customer journey templates stop at three or four stages and end at purchase. A travel map that ends at booking is missing the two stages, experiencing and sharing, that actually drive repeat business and referral in this category.

The non-linear part matters more than the stage names themselves. Phocuswright and Google's research found that over 40 percent of travellers move back and forth between dreaming and planning rather than progressing through them once, meaning a traveller can drift into planning-stage behaviour, checking specific dates and prices, then bounce back to dreaming-stage browsing of other destinations entirely before committing to either. A journey map that assumes a single forward pass through the stages will misread this back-and-forth as drop-off, when it is often just normal travel research behaviour that eventually converges on a booking.

This matters commercially because a mistimed stage read leads to the wrong fix. A team that sees a visitor return to the site three times without booking and reads that as a broken checkout will spend budget rebuilding a payment flow that was never the issue. The same pattern read correctly, as a traveller still mid-way through dreaming and planning rather than stalled at booking, points to a completely different fix: more decisive planning-stage content that helps them commit to this destination over the others they are still comparing.

How it works in practice. Mapping each stage properly.

StageWhat the traveller is doingWhat to map
DreamingBroad browsing, no destination fixed, often triggered by inspiration contentChannels: social, travel media, broad search. Content: destination inspiration, not pricing
PlanningDestination chosen, comparing dates, options and priceChannels: search, comparison sites, direct site visits. Content: practical detail, availability, entry requirements
BookingReady to purchase, comparing final price and termsChannels: direct site, OTA, phone or WhatsApp for higher-value trips. Content: clear pricing, cancellation terms, urgency signals
ExperiencingOn the trip, using confirmations, guides and support channelsChannels: email, app, on-property or in-destination support. Content: itinerary, local information, service recovery
SharingPost-trip reviews, photos and recommendationsChannels: review platforms, social, direct referral. Content: prompts to review, user-generated content capture

The mapping exercise itself should start from real data, not a workshop guess at what a traveller probably does. Search query data by stage is the most useful single input: broad, inspiration-led terms belong to dreaming, specific date-and-destination combinations belong to planning, and brand-plus-price searches belong to booking. Layering on-site behaviour, where visitors return to a page multiple times before converting, and post-trip review content completes the picture. A map built from a meeting room and a whiteboard, with no underlying search or booking data behind it, tends to describe what the team assumes rather than what travellers actually do.

Search intent shifts sharply between stages in a way that a single-page website often fails to reflect. A dreaming-stage visitor searching broad, inspirational terms who lands directly on a booking page with prices and a calendar has been handed content built for a different stage entirely, and will usually bounce. The map's job is to catch mismatches like this: which content exists for which stage, and where a stage has been left with nothing built for it at all.

Paid and organic channels tend to earn their keep at different stages, and the map should note this rather than treat every channel as relevant everywhere. Broad social and display advertising does most of its work at dreaming, where the goal is simply awareness of a destination or property, not an immediate conversion. Search advertising and organic search carry the heaviest weight at planning and booking, where the traveller already has intent and is comparing specific options. Email and app notifications belong almost entirely to experiencing and sharing, since by that point the traveller is already a customer rather than a prospect. Spending a paid media budget as if every channel converts equally at every stage is one of the more expensive assumptions a travel or hospitality marketing team can make.

Ownership of the map matters as much as its content. It should not sit solely with marketing, since booking-stage conversion issues are often a pricing, availability or checkout problem that marketing cannot fix alone, and post-trip sharing depends on how the on-property or in-destination team actually delivers the experience. A map reviewed jointly by marketing, revenue management and, where relevant, the operations team catches gaps a single department working alone tends to miss, particularly at the handoff points between stages.

What good looks like. A worked example.

Take an illustrative Gulf-based boutique hospitality group with this exact pattern. Their content was strong at the dreaming stage, striking photography, destination storytelling, a genuinely engaged social following, and strong again at booking, a clean, fast reservation flow. The planning stage in between had almost nothing built for it: no content on the practical questions an international traveller actually has once a destination is chosen, seasonal weather, visa and entry requirements, realistic total cost including transfers. Search data showed a consistent pattern of visitors arriving from dreaming-stage content, then leaving the site entirely rather than converting, most likely to search those practical questions elsewhere and often booking with whichever competitor answered them first.

The fix was not a redesign, it was a content gap closed at exactly one stage: a handful of planning-stage pages answering the specific practical questions the search data showed people asking, entry requirements by nationality, the best months to visit against local weather, a realistic total cost including transfers, linked directly from the dreaming-stage content that was already working. Direct booking conversions from organic traffic improved within the following quarter, not because the dreaming or booking stages changed at all, but because the middle of the journey stopped being a dead end.

The same exercise also flagged a smaller, easily missed gap at the sharing stage: guests were leaving reviews unprompted on third-party platforms, but nothing in the post-stay email sequence actually asked for one or pointed guests towards the channels the brand most wanted reviews on. Adding a single, well-timed request during the experiencing stage, close to check-out rather than days later, lifted the volume of reviews landing on the property's preferred platform, which matters because those reviews are themselves dreaming-stage content for the next traveller researching the same destination.

Pitfalls to avoid. Where travel journey maps go wrong.

The most common mistake is building the map once, in a workshop, and never checking it against real data again. Traveller origin markets shift, entry requirements change, and a new channel can gain share within a year, all of which move where and how people search at each stage. A map that was accurate eighteen months ago can misdirect content decisions today if no one has gone back to test it against current search and booking data.

The second common mistake is treating the five stages as a checklist to cover evenly rather than a diagnostic tool for finding the actual gap. Most travel and hospitality brands do not have five equally weak stages, they have one specific stage, usually planning, where the content simply does not exist yet. Spending equal effort rebuilding all five stages instead of fixing the one genuine gap wastes budget on stages that were never the problem.

The third mistake is stopping the map at booking altogether, which is still common among smaller operators who treat the reservation as the end of the customer relationship rather than the midpoint of it. Skipping experiencing and sharing means missing the two stages that cost the least to improve and produce content, reviews, photos, referrals, that feeds directly back into the next traveller's dreaming stage at effectively zero acquisition cost. A property that only ever markets to new dreaming-stage travellers, while ignoring the sharing-stage content its own recent guests are already producing, is paying full price for awareness it could partly be earning for free.

My rule for this work: build the map stage by stage against real search and channel data, not a workshop whiteboard guess. If you cannot point to the actual search term or channel a real traveller used to move from one stage to the next, the map is decoration, not a working tool, and it will not tell you where the booking is actually being lost.

Once the map exists in that form, revisiting it becomes a quick quarterly check rather than a fresh project each time: pull the same search and behaviour data, see whether the gap that was fixed last time has stayed fixed, and check whether a new one has opened up somewhere else in the five stages. That discipline, checked regularly against real data rather than redrawn from memory once a year, is what turns a journey map from a one-off workshop output into something the business actually uses to decide where to spend the next quarter's content and channel budget.

Common questions.

What are the five stages of a travel customer journey?

Dreaming, planning, booking, experiencing and sharing, a framework Google developed with the travel research firm Phocuswright. Dreaming is early-stage inspiration with no destination fixed yet, planning is narrowing down dates and options, booking is the purchase itself, experiencing is the trip, and sharing is the post-trip content that feeds the next traveller's dreaming stage.

How is a travel customer journey map different from a general customer journey map?

A general B2B customer journey map usually assumes a fairly linear path towards one purchase decision. A travel journey map has to account for a genuinely non-linear pattern, over 40 percent of travellers move back and forth between the dreaming and planning stages rather than progressing straight through, plus a distinct post-purchase experiencing and sharing phase that most B2B maps do not need.

What data should a travel customer journey map be built from?

Real search query data by stage, on-site behaviour showing where visitors return or abandon, booking funnel drop-off points, and post-trip review or social content, not a workshop guess at what travellers probably do. Search intent differs sharply by stage, from broad destination browsing to specific date and price comparisons, and the map should reflect that difference.

Where do most travel and hospitality brands lose customers in the journey?

Most commonly at the planning stage, where a traveller has narrowed down a destination but cannot find the specific, practical detail, entry requirements, best season, realistic pricing, that would move them towards booking. Brands that only publish inspirational dreaming-stage content and jump straight to a booking widget tend to lose travellers exactly at this gap.

How often should a travel customer journey map be updated?

At least annually, and sooner after any material change to booking behaviour, a new channel gaining share, a shift in traveller origin markets, or a change to visa or entry rules that affects the planning stage. A map built once and never revisited tends to drift away from how people are actually searching and booking within a year or two.

Does a small independent travel or hospitality business need a formal journey map?

Yes, though it does not need to be elaborate. Even a simple one-page version naming the content and channel for each of the five stages forces a small operator to notice gaps, usually at planning, that a bigger competitor with a dedicated marketing team has already closed.
