Process mapping & SOP creation

A warehouse SOP guide. Written for the floor, checked against a bad day.

Lauren PearsonFounder, The Pearson Co.2 November 20267 min read

Written by Lauren Pearson, Founder of The Pearson Co., a process mapping and SOP consultant for founder-led and operations-heavy teams. More on her background at the about section.

A warehouse SOP is a written, step-by-step instruction for one recurring task, receiving, put-away, picking, cycle counting or returns, covering the trigger, the sequence, the check, the safety step and who to escalate to. Most warehouse SOPs fail not because they're wrong on day one, but because nobody updates them once a scanner, a layout or a WMS changes.

The short answer. One task, one page, built around the exception.

A usable warehouse SOP covers five things: the trigger that starts the task, a purchase order arriving, a pick wave releasing, a cycle count scheduled, the steps in the order they're actually carried out, the check that confirms the task was done right, a quantity match, a location scan, a seal check, the safety step where one applies, and a clear line for who to call when something doesn't match. Warehousing and storage carried a total recordable injury and illness rate of 4.8 per 100 full-time workers in 2024, according to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics' Survey of Occupational Injuries and Illnesses, more than double the 2.3 rate across private industry overall. That gap is a reasonable argument on its own for why the safety step belongs in the document, not in a separate induction binder nobody reopens.

The warehouse SOPs that actually get followed tend to cover:

  • The trigger. What starts the task: a PO arriving at the dock, a pick wave releasing, a scheduled count, a return landing back on the receiving line.
  • The steps. Numbered, in the order the floor actually does them, including the scan or system step if one exists.
  • The check. What confirms the task was done correctly: a quantity match, a location confirmation, a pack weight check.
  • The safety step. PPE, powered equipment rules, or a hazard specific to that task, stated plainly rather than referenced from a general policy.
  • The escalation line. Exactly who to call when a count, a scan or a label doesn't match what the system expects.

Receiving and put-away sit at the top of that list for a reason: an error made there, a wrong quantity logged, an item shelved to the wrong bin, does not stay contained to that one task. It propagates into every pick against that location until a cycle count eventually catches the mismatch, by which point the warehouse may have shipped against stock it didn't actually have. Documenting those two tasks first, even before the more visible picking process, tends to remove more downstream error than starting with the task that feels most urgent on the floor that week.

How it works in practice. Write it for the worker holding the scanner, not the one who installed it.

The method starts the same way it does for any SOP: watch, or ask, the person who actually runs the task to walk through it step by step in the order they really do it, and note what they do when something is slightly off, a torn label, a quantity that doesn't match the manifest, a bin that's already full, before writing a single line. Our guide to creating an SOP covers that general method; this one is about what's different once a warehouse management system, scanning or automation sits between the worker and the task.

Zebra's 2025 Warehousing Vision Study, based on a survey of more than 1,700 warehouse associates and decision-makers, found that 63% of warehouse leaders plan to introduce AI software or augmented reality tools within five years, and that 89% of associates said they felt more valued when given technology and automation tools to do their job. Read together, those two figures point at a practical writing problem: an SOP drafted purely from how a task was done on paper a few years ago will not describe what a worker should actually do when a handheld device flags a mismatch, suggests an alternative bin location, or needs a manual override. The document has to be written for the job as it exists now, including the system step, not as a nostalgic description of a clipboard process the warehouse has already moved past.

Format should match the task's risk and repetition, not a single house style. A high-frequency manual task, picking, carton packing, works best as a laminated one-page job aid with photos of the exception cases kept at the station. A task tied to inventory accuracy or safety, cycle counting, powered equipment operation, hazardous goods handling, needs a longer document with a named owner, a revision number and a sign-off, because a shrinkage investigation or a safety audit will eventually ask which version was in force on a given shift.

What good looks like. It survives a new seasonal hire on a bad day.

Test a draft SOP on someone who has never run this specific task, ideally a recent or seasonal hire rather than a tenured one, and watch where they hesitate. Every point where they stop and look for a supervisor is a missing exception or a missing visual cue, not a sign the document needs more prose. A worker scanning a laminated sheet mid-shift is looking for the next action, not reading a paragraph, which is why photos and short numbered steps consistently outperform dense text in this setting.

It's also worth testing the document against a bad day rather than a smooth one. A receiving SOP that only works when the manifest matches the delivery exactly isn't describing the job as it's actually run; it's describing the one day out of twenty when nothing goes wrong, which is rarely the day anyone needs to open the document at all. The exception path, what to do when the count is short, the pallet is damaged, or the system shows a location that's physically full, is where a warehouse SOP earns its place on the shelf.

A practitioner's scenario. The SOP that worked on paper, and stopped working after the system change.

A warehouse operation I worked with, illustrative rather than a named result, had a put-away SOP that had served the team well for years: receive, inspect, scan, shelve to the suggested location, confirm. It read cleanly and every new hire was trained on it in their first week. The gap appeared only after the operation introduced a WMS upgrade that started suggesting overflow locations whenever a primary bin was full, a scenario the original SOP had never anticipated because the old system simply didn't make that suggestion. Workers handled the mismatch differently depending on who they asked, some shelved to the suggested overflow bin without question, some ignored the suggestion and used their own judgement, and a few quietly left pallets on the floor rather than decide. None of those responses was reckless; the document genuinely didn't tell them what the right answer was. Inventory accuracy on affected SKUs drifted for several weeks before a cycle count flagged the pattern. The fix wasn't a rewrite of the whole SOP, just one new line naming who confirms an overflow placement and how it gets logged, added the same week the gap was found. The decision rule that follows: whenever a system or a layout changes, the first question is not "does the SOP still read correctly" but "does it still name the exact decision a worker now has to make that didn't exist when it was written."

Pitfalls to avoid. Where warehouse SOPs quietly stop matching the floor.

  • Treating the SOP as a one-time project. A document written during a launch or an audit and never revisited drifts out of date the first time a layout, a supplier pack size or a system changes, usually within months rather than years.
  • Writing from the office rather than the dock or the aisle. An SOP drafted without watching the actual task run reliably misses the exception that matters most, the one the experienced worker handles automatically and never thinks to mention.
  • Leaving out the system step. Once a WMS, a scanner or a sortation system is involved, skipping what the worker does when the system disagrees with what they're looking at leaves exactly the gap described above.
  • No named owner or review date. A revision number and a named owner printed on the page do more to keep a warehouse SOP current than any policy stating documents should be reviewed regularly.

None of this requires documenting every task in the building at once. It requires starting with the task that causes the most disruption when the one person who knows it well is away, writing it from the floor rather than the office, and building in a line for what to do when the system and the physical shelf disagree. Common questions.

What should a warehouse SOP cover?

One task per document: the trigger that starts it, the exact sequence as it is actually performed, the check that confirms it was done correctly, the safety step, and who to call when a scan, a count or a location does not match. Most warehouse SOPs that fail are missing that last line.

Which warehouse tasks should get an SOP first?

Receiving and put-away, picking and pack verification, cycle counting, and returns processing, in roughly that order, because errors made at receiving or put-away propagate into every pick that follows. A task that depends on one experienced worker's memory, rather than a written step, is the one to prioritise regardless of which category it falls into.

Do warehouse SOPs need to change once a WMS or scanning is introduced?

Yes. A paper-era SOP written around a packing slip and a clipboard does not describe what a worker should do when a handheld scanner flags a mismatch, which location to try next, or when to override a system suggestion, so it stops matching the actual job the day new technology goes live, not before.

How often should a warehouse SOP be reviewed?

On a fixed schedule, commonly every six to twelve months, and immediately after any change to the WMS, the floor layout, or the equipment used for that task. An SOP with no named owner and no review date is the one most likely to still describe a layout that no longer exists.

How detailed should a warehouse SOP be?

Detailed enough that a temporary or newly hired worker can complete the task correctly without finding a supervisor, and no more. A routine pick or put-away task usually needs one page with photos of the exception cases; a safety-critical task, such as powered equipment operation, needs a longer document with a sign-off.
